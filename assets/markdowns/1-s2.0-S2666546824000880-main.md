A novel approach for generative time series simulation of electricity price scenarios is presented. A ‘‘Time Series Simulation Conditional Generative Adversarial Network’’ (TSS-CGAN) generates short-term electricity price scenarios. In particular, the network is capable of generating a 24-dimensional output vector that corresponds to the expected behavior of electricity markets. The model can replace typical approaches from financial mathematics like statistical factor models to model the price distribution around a given forecast. The data cover a 3-year period from 2020 to 2023. Our empirical study is conducted on the EPEX SPOT market in Europe. An electricity price scenario includes the prices of the hourly contracts of a day-ahead auction at the EPEX SPOT power exchange. The model uses multivariate time series as input factors, consisting of point forecasts of electricity prices and fundamental data on generation and load profiles. The architecture of a TSSCGAN is based on the idea of Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks combined with 1D Convolutional Neural Networks and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory. The model is evaluated using qualitative and quantitative criteria. For the evaluation, 10,000 simulations of a test period are carried out. Qualitative criteria are whether the model follows certain electricity market-specific regularities and depicts them adequately. The quantitative analysis includes common error metric, compared to benchmark models, like DeepAR, Prophet and Temporal Fusion Transformer, the examination of the quantile ranges, the error distribution and a sensitivity analysis. The results show that the TSS-CGAN outperforms benchmark models such as DeepAR by reducing the continuous ranked probability score by $5 0 \%$ and considers market-specific circumstances such as the production of fluctuating energies and reacts correctly to changes in the corresponding variables.

# 1. Introduction

Electricity has been an exchange-traded product in most countries of the world for more than 30 years. It can be traded on different time scales (forward, day-ahead, intraday), usually with day-ahead as the reference market. Important examples are the European dayahead markets on EPEX Spot, NORDPOOL in the Nordic countries and PJM or Nodal Exchange in the US. Each electricity market has its own unique characteristics. For example, the German market is dominated by volatile renewable generation from wind and solar energy, the French market consists mainly of large nuclear power plants, and NORDPOOL is dominated by hydro power. The composition of generation is crucial to the price dynamics of different markets. The aim of this work is to develop a generative model capable of generating realistic electricity price scenarios of a day-ahead market, which could replace classical probabilistic models and their usually very complex calibration [1]. A purely data-driven model can enable the analyst to adequately model different markets without a thorough statistical analysis. In this paper, we use the German day-ahead market as a reference for our case study.

# 1.1. Electricity pricing on the day-ahead market

The day-ahead electricity price is an important indicator for the energy market. It indicates the price for electrical energy that is set for the delivery of electricity on the next day. This subsection deals with how the day-ahead electricity price is formed, which factors influence it and which market mechanisms are at work. Important exchanges and market players that play a role in price formation are also highlighted.

The day-ahead electricity price is determined on the spot market of the EPEX SPOT (European Power Exchange), where 13 nations on the European continent trade energy. Prices are determined through an auction process in which supply meets demand. The auction takes place every day of the year at 12:00 noon (CET/CEST) and sets prices for each hour of the following day. The prices are valid for each hour of the day in advance (ex ante), i.e. the price at 00:00 applies to the period between 00:00 and 01:00. Market participants submit their bids by 12:00 (CET/CEST). These bids reflect the willingness to buy or sell a certain quantity at a certain time. The exchange aggregates a demand curve from the buy orders and a supply curve from the sell orders. Both curves are determined for each delivery period (i.e. each of the $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ of the day). The intersection at which supply and demand come to coincide results in the market clearing price (MCP), which applies to all buyers and sellers. As a result of this order matching, the power exchange establishes legally binding agreements to buy or sell a certain amount of electricity in a given delivery area at the agreed price. Pricing is based on the merit order principle. This principle guarantees the lowest possible prices to meet demand in the electricity market, as the generation with the lowest cost (or willingness to sell at the lowest price) comes first. This creates an ‘order of advantage’ or ‘merit order’.

The day-ahead electricity price in Germany is shaped by multiple factors. Weather conditions significantly impact renewable energy generation, with strong winds or sunshine lowering prices and adverse conditions raising them due to reliance on costly fossil fuels. Additionally, fossil fuel costs, including coal, natural gas, and oil, affect electricity generation costs, influencing the day-ahead price.

The special feature of the German spot market for electricity is that it is influenced by a high proportion of renewable energy in electricity generation $5 6 \%$ in 2023 [2]). This influence can be seen in the electricity price as shown in Fig. 1. Further examples and influences on the electricity price are the subject of Section 4.

The simulation of uncertain electricity price scenarios is of interest to various parties in the energy industry. Suppliers use simulation models to optimize the design of their power plants by means of stochastic optimization [3]. Risk managers utilize simulations to quantify short-term risk, whereas retailers use them daily to optimize their portfolios [4].

In practice, quite accurate spot forecasts are also available, usually purchased from different suppliers. For the above applications, especially with a flexible portfolio (e.g. hydro), the distribution around the point forecasts is of particular interest. These could be generated from fundamental models with stochastic input [5], or from stochastic price models (references in Section 2). In either case, this process requires either crucial and often unrealistic assumptions or a lot of historical data and very detailed market modeling. Our approach, based on generative models, minimizes the need for assumptions and historical analysis and learns the properties of the market from historical data. These learned properties are then used to generate simulations.

# 1.2. Generative time series simulation

Generative Deep Learning has gained a lot of attention in recent years [6]. Especially in the area of image and text processing, generative modeling finds the most diverse use cases [7]. Deep fakes or intelligent voice assistants are ubiquitous and enjoy as many supporters as critics. Generative models have the property of reproducing complex data structures such as images or spoken sentences. The generated data resembles training data, but is random and unique. Generative modeling is to be distinguished from discriminative modeling. Discriminative modeling is associated with supervised learning, that is, learning a function that maps an input to an output using a labeled data set. Generative modeling techniques usually do not require labels and are therefore a form of unsupervised learning. GANs meanwhile became an established tool in the generation of synthetic data. Synthetic data are artificially generated and have structural and statistical similarities compared to the original data. For example, GANs are excellent at generating deceptively real-looking images where a human cannot tell whether they are real or artificially generated. Images are typically encoded as a 4D tensor (one image as a 3D tensor) [8]. In the recent past, GANs have also been successfully used in the synthesis of tensors of lower dimensions. Especially the modeling of time series is of interest. Whether sensor data [9], stock indices [10] or solar system performance [11], the use of GANs to model time series is becoming increasingly popular.

The contributions to this paper can be summarized as follows:

• We present a CGAN-based architecture that simulates realistic electricity price scenarios, incorporating forecasts of energymarket specific features (Section 3).   
• We propose a method to evaluate such a generative network under the energy market specific aspects and to quantify the risk of a given price forecast by a distribution (Section 4).   
• We highlight the strengths and weaknesses of the architecture and compare its performance to that of established probabilistic models including DeepAR, Prophet, and Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) (Section 5).

# 2. Related work

# 2.1. Generative time series simulation

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), first introduced by Ian Goodfellow et al. (2014), are a special neural network architecture consisting of two competing components: the generator and the discriminator. The generator creates new data using random input vectors, whereas the discriminator attempts to distinguish between generated data and real data. The goal of the generator is to fool the discriminator into falsely classifying a generated data set as real. Through competition and iterative training between the generator and discriminator, GANs can generate realistic and high-quality data that resembles real data.

At the end of 2014, Mehdi Mirza and Simon Osindero extended the architecture developed by Goodfellow with a multimodal approach [12]. Conditional GANs (CGANs) can be used to generate images depending on a condition. For this purpose, the generator is given a certain feature as input, depending on which output is generated. The first CGAN presented by Mirza and Osindero used the MNIST dataset as an example. The CGAN is capable of generating pictures of handwritten digits depending on the input passed. Here, the generator implicitly learns the distribution of the original data via the loss function of the discriminator, which is trained with both real and fake data.

GANs have also been successfully used recently to generate time series of various types. A well-known work on this was published in 2019 by Jinsung Yoon et al. [13]. The work focuses on the generation of multivariate time series and also considers the correlations among variables over time in the generation process [13]. A completely new approach in generating time series with GANs was presented by Alireza Koochali et al. also in 2019. They developed an architecture that allows a GAN to be trained to generate one future time step depending on the last $K$ time steps [9]. The architecture is based on CGANs because the last $K$ time steps enter the generator as a condition. However, this approach not only has the limitation of generating only one time step, but it only considers univariate time series as condition. When modeling electricity prices, it is advantageous to resort to multivariate time series and include them as covariates in the modeling. The integration of additional features, such as weather-dependent output from renewable energies [14], demand [15] or fossil fuels [16] to map complex non-linear relationships, is common.

In 2021, Koochali’s approach was extended by Gerardo Zúñiga and Gonzalo Acuña to include generation of up to 5 time steps $t + K ( 0 <$ $K < 6 )$ [17]. Thus, there was still the problem that this architecture only considers univariate time series as condition input. Milena Vuletic et al. (2021) applied the work of Koochali et al. to financial time series and used a specially developed loss function that takes profit and loss as well as the sharp ratio into account [18].

Xiaoqiao Huang et al. (2022) developed an architecture that forecast one time step of solar power output, depending on the last $K$ time steps of a dependent target variable and a feature matrix of covariates refering to the same time steps [11]. Both (last $K$ time steps and feature matrix) entered the network as conditions, which represents a significant further development compared to Koochali’s univariate approach.

In the area of electricity price scenario simulation, there is already relevant preliminary work that can be built upon. First, Zhang et al. (2021) proposed and verified the effectiveness of GANs for predicting marginal prices in wholesale electricity markets [19]. Demir et al. (2021) proposed a Wasserstein-GAN (WGAN) for data augmentation in day-ahead price forecasting regression tasks, which can reduce regression errors [20]. Xin Liu et al. (2022) have applied GAN-based models to day-ahead electricity price prediction in Australia for the first time. The work is based on the TimeGAN [13] and takes into account historical time series of weather conditions and realized prices, using Wasserstein distance as loss metric [21]. The work was extended in 2023 to improve the quality of the predictions and the stability of the training [22]. However, the application of Wasserstein loss leads to some challenges, such as gradient clipping, whose effects have not yet been sufficiently investigated. Furthermore, the complex architecture of TimeGANs leads to very high computational costs, which is why this work is orientated towards a more classical CGAN architecture with binary cross entropy as a loss function, as was done in [11]. In addition, classic CGAN-based architectures achieve comparable results to the WGAN approaches, as a study on day-ahead energy demand forecasting showed [23].

The aim and main contribution of this paper is to propose a CGANbased architecture that considers multivariate time series as input as in [11] and simulates all $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ of the auction at once. Among other things, highly accurate deterministic point forecasts will be used for this purpose, as described in [24]. The authors of this paper used highly accurate deterministic point forecasts of the dependent variable as input and trained a probabilistic model that predicts the distribution. This corresponds exactly to the use case of this work, in which energy managers purchase forecasts and want to output a distribution using generative methods. And finally the architecture should generate a univariate time series consisting of several contiguous time steps as in [17].

# 2.2. Energy industry

Various approaches to simulating uncertain day-ahead electricity price scenarios have been pursued in the energy industry. Some of the methods used come from the field of financial mathematics, which seems appropriate for this specific application due to its flexibility and adaptability.

One common method is the application of stochastic models, in particular the use of stochastic differential equations (SDGs) for modeling price scenarios, as Eduardo Schwartz and James E. Smith did in 2000 [25]. These models take into account uncertainties and fluctuations in various market parameters and enable the generation of simulations for day-ahead electricity prices. Such approaches make it possible to simulate future price developments.

Various extensions and alternatives to the Schwartz-Smith model have been proposed. We refer to [1] for an overview on factor models and an application of factor models on the German electricity market. On the other extreme, the energy industry uses fundamental models. These are models packed with detailed data about the market. For each delivery period, those models solve an optimization problem minimizing the total cost of supply. Due to their computational complexity, fundamental models are not suited for the simulation of thousands of price paths. The data and the models are usually held by consultancies, which sell the model itself or forecasted price scenarios under certain assumptions.

Generative neural networks offer an alternative to existing price simulation techniques due to the ability to learn patterns and structures from data. The use of discriminative neural networks in the energy market context has already been established in numerous studies, which clearly qualifies the technology as a potential tool, like in [26] or [27]. Generative neural networks, by contrast, are just entering the energy sector. Recent research in this field has also produced review articles, such as the one by Yilmaz et al. [28], who evaluated different GAN architectures using Turkish intraday electricity prices. Hanif et al. [29] demonstrated the superiority of GANs over models like Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) in 2023, using Canadian electricity prices as an example. Most recently, Wang et al. [30] introduced a GAN for stock price prediction in the area of factor investment.

# 3. Methodology

In the area of time series with neural networks special architectures are established, which will be discussed in more detail in the following subsections. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are standard architectures when it comes to processing time series with neural networks. Both architectures can be found in numerous industrial applications, including the financial sector and the energy industry [31]. The following Sections 3.1 and 3.2 deal with the architecture of 1D Convolutional Layer and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM - they are also part of the recurrent networks). Simple recurrent networks are not of interest because they have only a very limited short-term memory, whereas the more complex Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cells can also deal well with long-term dependencies and vanishing gradients.

# 3.1. 1D convolutional layer

Convolutional layers, integral to CNNs, operate differently from fully connected neural networks.

Neurons in each layer connect to a subset of the previous layer (referred to as a local receptive field), capturing small-scale patterns like corners and edges (using pictures as an example). Deeper layers assemble these patterns into higher-level concepts, creating a spatial hierarchical structure. CNNs show some invariance to displacement, distortion, or scaling, so they recognizing patterns despite variations. Unlike fully connected networks, CNNs do not rely on fixed pixel arrangements. The concept extends to higher-dimensional problems (e.g., spatial structures) and lower dimensions (e.g., time series).

1D CNNs have proven to be extremely effective models for processing time series [32]. Their success lies in their ability to recognize local patterns and temporal dependencies in data. By applying convolutional layers, 1D CNNs can automatically extract relevant features, utilizing the spatial relationships between successive points in time. A 1D max pooling layer follows the convolutional layers, further enhancing the model’s performance by downsampling the input, which reduces its dimensionality and computational load. This layer effectively captures the most significant features by selecting the maximum value within a specified window, preserving the most prominent patterns while discarding less important information. This enables efficient detection of patterns and trends in time series data, which is particularly useful in applications such as financial forecasting, climate modeling and signal processing [33].

The use of convolutional layers in GANs to model time series is common practice, for example in short-term load forecasting [34] or day-ahead wind power prediction [35].

# 3.2. Bidirectional long short-term memory

Long Short-Term Memory layers [36] are another standard tool in deep learning for time series forecasting, especially due to their ability to capture temporal dependencies. LSTMs store relevant information about past states, which increases their adaptability to sequences of different lengths. This enables modeling of complex time courses and the capture of long and short-term dependencies in the data. LSTM layers in GANs are used in complex prediction problems such as stock price prediction [37].

Bidirectional LSTM layers, founded in 2005 by Graves and Schmidhuber [38] allow the network to look both forwards and backwards in time and thereby develop a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying patterns. This makes bidirectional LSTM layers particularly more accurate for applications which require a holistic analysis of longterm temporal dependencies, such as in financial analysis or language processing, as Siami-Namini et al. showed [39]. This approach is based on the assumption that a bidirectional RNN can identify patterns that a unidirectional RNN might miss. Graves and Schmidhuber demonstrated its effectiveness by comparing two architectures — classic RNN and LSTM — both in unidirectional and bidirectional forms using the TIMIT data set as an example, which contains broadband recordings of 630 speakers of eight major dialects of American English, each reading ten phonetically rich sentences [40]. The bidirectional versions not only converged in half the epochs but also achieved up to $5 \%$ higher accuracy on test data. Bidirectional RNNs are also used in natural language processing (NLP), where word order dependency is not unidirectional. For example, the meaning of a word in a sentence may depend on the meaning of a word that appears much later in the sentence. On the electricity market, this reversed order dependency in which an earlier point in time depends on a later point in time can also be observed. For example, a high wind forecast in the afternoon can also have an effect on prices in the early morning hours. The operators know that they have to bridge a short time until they can produce cheap renewable electricity, so they would not switch on expensive inflexible power plants (like coal), as these have a minimum run time. Instead, they would opt for more flexible but more expensive energy sources such as gas turbines. The key advantage of bidirectional LSTM networks lies in their ability to learn an additional representation of the data, providing a second perspective. Siami-Namini et al. [39] compared LSTM and Bi-LSTM in stock market predictions, showing that Bi-LSTM networks were on average $3 8 \%$ more accurate, attributed to their ability to reveal backward temporal structures that unidirectional models might miss. In addition to LSTM/Bi-LSTM cells, there is the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), considered a simplified version of the LSTM cell [41]. A direct comparison of LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and GRU was conducted by Seabe et al. [42] using cryptocurrency price prediction, where Bi-LSTM outperformed GRU and LSTM across different currencies. In the field of energy consumption forecasting, Bi-LSTM cells have also proven to be superior [43]. The Bi-LSTM layers are also used to solve highdimensional problems in the field of time series prediction, e.g. for the prediction or renewable energy production, like [44] for wind and [11] for solar photovoltaic (PV) power. For the exact functionality of 1D CNNs and Bi-LSTM layers, please refer to the standard literature, François Chollet [8] or Aurélien Géron [45].

# 3.3. Generative adversarial networks

The model architecture of a GAN typically consists of two interconnected artificial neural networks, a generative and a discriminative model (generator and discriminator). Fig. 2 shows the relationship between generator and discriminator.

The input layer of the generator takes a noise in the form of a random normally distributed vector as a 1D tensor and processes it into an output according to its weights. This vector describes a point of an $n$ -dimensional latent space. A latent space is a low-dimensional representation of a high-dimensional sample space. The generator can be understood as a function $G ( z )$ that assigns a latent space vector $z$ of distribution $p _ { z }$ to a high-level representation. The discriminator, as a function $D ( x )$ , estimates the probability that an observation $x$ belongs to the distribution $p _ { \mathtt { d a t a } }$ (original data) [46]. Consequently, the discriminator is modeled as a binary classifier and trained to distinguish the generated data from the real data.

The cross entropy of the $\mathrm { i } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ observation can be written in general terms for the binary case as:

$$
S ( y _ { i } , \hat { y } _ { i } ) = - [ y _ { i } \log ( \hat { y } _ { i } ) + ( 1 - y _ { i } ) \log ( 1 - \hat { y } _ { i } ) ] ,
$$

where $y$ represents the truth content of the feature $( y = 1$ for true or $y = 0$ for false) and $\hat { y }$ represents the predicted probability of observing this feature. The generator and discriminator pursue different goals, whereas the discriminator attempts to correctly classify data as real or fake, the generator attempts to mislead the discriminator into a false classification.

For a batch of observations the average observed discriminator and generator loss minimization can be described as follows:

Discriminator loss minimization $\operatorname* { m i n } _ { D } \Big [ - \Big ( \mathbb { E } _ { x \sim p _ { \mathrm { d a t a } } } [ \log ( D ( x ) ) ] + \mathbb { E } _ { z \sim p _ { z } } [ \log ( 1 - D ( G ( z ) ) ) ] \Big ) \Big ]$

# Generator loss minimization

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { G } \left[ - \left( \mathbb { E } _ { z \sim p _ { z } } [ \log ( D ( G ( z ) ) ) ] \right) \right]
$$

The global optimum is reached when $p _ { g } = p _ { \mathrm { d a t a } }$ , so the discriminator can no longer distinguish between the two distributions, i.e. $D ( x ) =$ 0.5 [46]. Inserted into the loss function of the discriminator, the optimal loss at convergence is given by $- \log ( 0 . 5 ) - \log ( 0 . 5 ) \approx 0 . 6 .$ . Furthermore, this architecture has the following features:

• The generator learns the real data, without ever having seen it, implicitly via the information contained in the gradients. • The training process of the network resembles a zero-sum game since a reduction in the loss of the generator automatically leads to a higher loss of the discriminator and vice versa. The generator gets better at generating synthetic data that the discriminator misclassifies as genuine, whereas the discriminator learns with each misclassification, which in turn leads the generator to generate even more suitable data. • The training of the generator and discriminator is done simultaneously and not sequentially, i.e., both generator and discriminator are trained within one training iteration. Updating of the generator weights takes place under frozen discriminator weights since the gradients are first propagated through the discriminator before reaching the generator weights. The freezing is necessary because the generator and discriminator use different loss functions. Otherwise, the generator could get better by degrading the discriminator, not because the generator actually gets better at generating data from the distribution $p _ { \mathtt { d a t a } }$ .

# 3.4. Conditional generative adversarial networks

Although Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks were presented in the same year as the basic architecture, they still represent a milestone in GAN research. The developers of the first GAN [46] already pointed out, in the outlook of their work, that the architecture can be extended by a further input, which both generator and discriminator receive, demonstrated in Fig. 3.

This input can be understood as a condition to influence the output of the generator. For example, such a condition could be a class label that contains more detailed information about a data point [12]. This class label enters the network as a one-hot encoded vector. The discriminator is trained on both synthetic and real data with an appropriate label vector. This point is particularly important because otherwise the discriminator cannot judge whether a synthetic data set matches a label or not. The identical logic must be applied for training with real data. The loss functions for the generator and the discriminator can be adopted according to the basic architecture from Section 3.3 with the adjustment $D ( x | y )$ for the probability of the discriminator to detect an observation $x$ under condition $y$ and $G ( z | y )$ for the mapping from z to $p _ { g }$ under condition y, respectively.

A special feature of this architecture is the so-called concatenate layer. This layer is used to connect a label tensor with a noise tensor and a label tensor with a synthetic or real data tensor. Mathematically, this operation can be described as follows: Let ${ \vec { z } } = ( z _ { 1 } , z _ { 2 } , z _ { 3 } , \ldots , z _ { 1 0 0 } )$ be a latent space vector and ${ \vec { x } } \ = \ ( x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , x _ { 3 } , \ldots , x _ { 1 0 } )$ be a label vector representative of 10 mutually exclusive features. A conjunction of both vectors encoded as a 1D tensor yields the 1D tensor $\begin{array} { r l } { { \vec { c } } } & { { } = } \end{array}$ $( z _ { 1 } , z _ { 2 } , z _ { 3 } , \ldots , z _ { 1 0 0 } , x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , x _ { 3 } , \ldots , x _ { 1 0 } )$ . This logic can also be applied to higher dimensional tensors. To connect the tensors, it is important that they have the same dimension in all spatial directions except one. The idea of a generator that produces outputs under a condition allows the use of GANs for time series synthesis under certain conditions, such as the values of the last time steps, as Koochali et al. did [9].

# 3.5. Time series simulation conditional generative adversarial network

This subsection is the core of this paper. We propose a CGAN-based model architecture for probabilistic simulation of uncertain day-ahead electricity price scenarios. The core architecture of this work is inspired by Huang et al. [11]. We extend and adapt the architecture and training to include the following points:

![](images/6073f6e5342c6c134efcf8b81abed4a3a8c38529204e158b9b6251152362b1ee.jpg)  
Fig. 4. Model architecture of the generator of a TSS-CGAN.

• The output of the generator corresponds to a 24-dimensional vector instead of just a single scalar. This takes into account the temporal order of an electricity price curve and brings the individual time steps into a relationship with each other. • The authors of [11] include the target variable twice in the feature matrix before it passes through the Bi-LSTM layer. We suggest including the target variable only once in the feature matrix, which balances the weighting of the other features. • We pre-train the part of the generator without the CNN layers and the random noise as a supervised learning problem for 10,000 iterations to improve the prediction accuracy. • We increased the update frequency of the discriminator weights compared to the generator weights to a ratio of 2:1 in order to be able to classify the electricity price curve more precisely.

The exact architecture is explained in the following. The price $\tilde { y }$ of the next $2 4 \ \mathrm { ~ h ~ }$ is generated. Starting from a time ??, the prices $\tilde { y } _ { t + 1 } , \tilde { y } _ { t + 2 } , \dots , \tilde { y } _ { t + 2 4 }$ are of interest. The realized prices $y _ { t - 2 3 } , y _ { t - 2 2 } , \ldots , y _ { t - 1 } , y _ { t }$ of the last $2 4 \ \mathrm { h }$ enter the network as a condition. It depends on the market under consideration, which fundamental factors are sensible to use. In our example, the German market, forecasts of wind and solar production are relevant. Other factors such as the availability of conventional power plants, energy commodity prices, etc. are also useful. These fundamental data refer to the time points $t + 1$ to $t + 2 4$ and are available as forecasts. To stabilize the training, a price forecast is included which refers to the same period as the fundamental data. Another relevant input factor is the hour to which a data point refers, expressed as a positive integer between 0 and 23. The price forecast, the fundamental forecasts, and the hour enter the network as a feature matrix. This feature matrix is described as

$$
X = \left[ \begin{array} { c c c c c } { P r i c e _ { t + 1 } } & { P r i c e _ { t + 2 } } & { . . . } & { P r i c e _ { t + 2 4 } } \\ { S o l a r _ { t + 1 } } & { S o l a r _ { t + 2 } } & { . . . } & { S o l a r _ { t + 2 4 } } \\ { W i n d _ { t + 1 } } & { W i n d _ { t + 2 } } & { . . . } & { W i n d _ { t + 2 4 } } \\ { \vdots } & { \vdots } & { \ddots } & { \vdots } \\ { H o u r _ { t + 1 } } & { H o u r _ { t + 2 } } & { . . . } & { H o u r _ { t + 2 4 } } \end{array} \right] .
$$

The generator is therefore a function $G ( z , X , y )$ that, using a random normally distributed vector $z$ , a vector $y _ { : }$ , and a feature matrix $X$ , generates an electricity price scenario $\tilde { y }$ . The discriminator requires the same conditions as the generator to assess whether an electricity price scenario appears realistic given past electricity price scenarios and their associated conditions. In this respect, the output of the discriminator can be written down as a function $D ( \hat { y } , X , y )$ , where $\hat { y }$ represents a realized electricity price scenario. Thus, the output of the discriminator for a generated electricity price scenario would be $D ( \tilde { y } , X , y )$ , for $\tilde { y } =$ $G ( z , X , y )$ . The loss is determined using the binary cross entropy. The architecture has some special features and is explained in Fig. 4.

The dimension of the latent space vector is part of current research [47] and has a significant influence on the generalization capability of the model. Since we intend to fit the high-dimensional data to a low-dimensional distribution, we want the dimension of the noise to be as small as possible without losing relevant information. The optimal noise dimension depends on the problem to be solved and varies greatly from case to case [47]. In the case of TSS-CGANs, the dimension of the latent vector $z$ depends on the number of features that are constrained. The vector $z$ , after passing through two 1D convolutional and 1D max pooling layers, is reshaped into the same matrix form as $X$ to perform element-wise multiplication to merge the latent space vector and the condition matrix. The reshaping is illustrated in Fig. 4 for the generator and in Fig. 5 for the discriminator and takes place before the CNN layers are passed. The element-wise multiplication is marked as a circled $\mathbf { X }$ in both figures and connects the reshaping with the feature matrix. Element-by-element multiplication is critical here because an arithmetic operation directly relates the properties of the latent space to the predicted conditions of the scenario being simulated. The resulting matrix is of the form $( 2 4 , n )$ , where $n$ is the number of features in the feature matrix. The realized prices of the last 24 time steps are concatenated (illustrated as a circled cross in Figs. 4 and 5) to the matrix as an additional feature, along the feature axis. The resulting $( 2 4 , n + 1 )$ matrix passes through a Bi-LSTM layer, to extract temporal features, followed by a Dense layer (corresponds to a fully connected layer, where all neurons of a layers are connected to all neurons of a previous layer), which outputs a 24-dimensional vector. The peculiarity of this architecture arises from the inclusion of a feature matrix as well as their underlying temporal structure. The combination of Bi-LSTM layer as well as 1D CNN layer plays a crucial role in the quality of the generated scenarios, because the Bi-LSTM layer extracts the temporal structure of the sequence, whereas the 1D CNN layers extract the spatial features of the latent space.

![](images/8073c72b934dd82f1afc8e35ffbb9ed241331649e2dcc2d37382eae016b8bca4.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Model architecture of the discriminator of a TSS-CGAN.

The discriminator has a similar structure, with the difference that instead of the latent space vector $z$ , the previously generated prices are passed through the 1D convolutional layer to extract the spatial features and then multiplied elementwise by the feature matrix. A second BiLSTM layer is also used, which processes the generated electricity price scenario and its realized previous electricity prices as a sequence. The results of both Bi-LSTM layers are concatenated and output behind a Dense layer. Only the output layer of the discriminator uses a logistic activation function, whereas all other layers of the network use the ReLU activation function. The ReLU activation function (Rectified Linear Unit) sets all negative input values to zero and passes positive input values unchanged, introducing non-linearity into the model while simplifying the computation. The architecture of the discriminator is shown in Fig. 5.

The number of neurons in the layers depends on the number of features to be considered and the number of time steps considered. For the number of filters in the two 1D convolutional layers as well as the Bi-LSTM layers, we follow the approach of Huang et al. [11]. The first 1D convolutional layer has 32 filters, and the second has 64 filters. The kernel size is 2 (also in the max pooling layer). The number of Bi-LSTM units is 24.

# 4. Evaluation

The TSS-CGAN will be applied as a probabilistic model to simulate electricity price scenarios. The evaluation of such a model can be done in different ways. Realistic electricity price scenarios can be evaluated qualitatively by looking at particular characteristics such as seasonality over the course of the day and week. A probabilistic model should also have a realistic probability distribution of the simulated prices. This can be achieved by a quantitative analysis of the quantiles as well as the distribution of differences between real price and generated price. Finally, the model is tested with respect to the variation of electricity market specific variables. A sensitivity analysis provides information on whether the model reacts correctly to the characteristic mechanisms of the electricity market.

The implementation was carried out on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 7975WX 32-Core CPU, 128 GB of RAM, and 2x NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs. In the real-world implementation, model training and sensitivity analysis are particularly computationally intensive, each taking approximately half a day with the aforementioned setup. The modeling was conducted using the TensorFlow/Keras library (version 2.13) with Python 3.10.

A data set with 30,073 data points is available for model training and evaluation. The period under consideration covers 3, 5 years from 17 June 2020 to 21 November 2023. The data consists of point forecasts of the hourly day-ahead electricity price (mean absolute error (MAE): $1 0 . 1 2 ~ \in \mathrm { / M W h }$ ) and electricity market-specific fundamental data (generation from solar and wind, available capacity of gas, lignite, hard coal and nuclear power, demand and required residual load) and refers to the EPEX SPOT power exchange, more precisely to the market area Germany-Luxembourg. The realized day-ahead electricity prices to which the forecasts refer are also available. Another feature is the hour to which the values refer, as a positive integer from 0 to 23. In total, there are 9 features:

• Price scenario forecast • Previous day price scenario • Solar energy forecast • Wind energy forecast • Residual load forecast • Demand forecast • Gas capacity forecast • Coal capacity forecast • Hour

![](images/f2a136426bca4e1382d8116dcd630ef8d2c9ee44e9109a49fd146d09f8e24b35.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Characteristic day-ahead electricity price curve with high share of renewable energy.

The forecast data exhibit certain inconsistencies. For example, there are gaps in the data that must be accounted for during model training. When capacity values for coal and gas are missing, they are imputed using daily averages, provided that all other features are present. This approximation is negligible because the capacity values are not as volatile as, for instance, the price forecasts themselves. Gaps in the price forecasts are linearly interpolated. Days where at least one column has 12 or more missing values are completely removed to avoid introducing bias into the data. Additionally, the feature matrix is normalized using min–max scaling. The model was trained for the period from 17 June 2020 to 21 November 2022 and evaluated in the period from 22 November 2022 to 21 November 2023. A special characteristic of this period is the high level of uncertainty on the markets caused by pandemics and political conflicts. Other hyperparameters include:

• Training iterations: 50,000   
• Discriminator train frequency per iteration: 2   
• Batch size: 500   
• Optimizer: Adam (learning rate $= 0 . 0 0 0 1 $ )   
• Activation Functions: ReLU (output layer of the discriminator: sigmoid)   
• Loss function: Binary cross entropy   
• Loss function (pre-train generator): Mean squared error (MSE)

For the learning rate, batch size, optimizer, and discriminator loss function, we followed the hyperparameters used by Huang et al. [11]. However, for the number of training iterations, we based our decision on the model’s convergence, which we determined by observing the stabilization of the discriminator loss. We experimented with different numbers of discriminator weight updates per iteration, and found that two updates per iteration produced the best results according to our criteria.

After the successful training of the GAN, we run the generator to generate 10,000 simulations on the test period. A single simulation generates 365 24-hour scenarios. All 24-hour scenarios from one simulation together result in the test period and use the identical latent space vector for the entire test period to bring the electricity price scenarios into a mathematical context.

# 4.1. Qualitative analysis

A single electricity price scenario for the day-ahead market consists of 24 values with the first price for the delivery period from 00:00 to 00:59 and the last price for the delivery period from 23:00 to 23:59. The combination of all 24 values results in a curve that exhibits certain regularities. Higher generation from renewable energies reduces the share of the required conventional production, which reduces the electricity price via the merit order principle. Very sunny days lead to the characteristic ‘‘camel shape’’ of the electricity price curve. Days with a low share of renewable energies and a high demand also show a characteristic curve. Due to the lower electricity consumption at weekends, a decline in demand for short-term energy can be seen, which reduces the overall price.

# 4.1.1. Days with a high share of renewable energy

Solar energy generated during the day reduces the share of residual load required. On sunny days, the electricity price decreases during the midday peak, whereas it increases during the early morning and late afternoon hours due to the higher residual load. Fig. 6 shows an electricity price curve for Monday, September 5, 2022, which clearly illustrates the mechanism described above. The horizontal axis shows the hours of the day described. The left vertical axis refers to the electricity price in $\mathbf { \oplus / M W h }$ , whereas the right vertical axis refers to the load (solar and wind) in MW and the residual load in MW, respectively.

# 4.1.2. Days with low share of renewables

If the share of renewables in the overall coverage of electricity consumption is low, this is also reflected in the electricity price curve, as in this case it is mainly conventional fossil fuels that contribute to the coverage of consumption. This leads to a stronger increase in the price in the morning hours when consumption increases, or to a decrease in the price when consumption decreases in the evening. Since there are no fluctuating energy sources feeding into the grid, the curve runs with much less volatility during the peak load period. Fig. 7 shows an electricity price curve for Friday, December 9, 2022 with a small amount of renewable energy meeting electricity demand and illustrates this behavior.

# 4.1.3. Seasonalities in day and week

Since supply and demand determine the price to a large extent, the price is significantly lower on weekends due to lower demand from industry. Demand is also lower at night, resulting in a lower price than during the day. Fig. 1 shows an electricity price curve from Monday, November 28, 2022 to Sunday, December 4, 2022.

The seasonal behavior is clearly visible, with prices reaching their low point around 00:00 and then rising again. The right side of the figure shows the weekend with the Friday 20:00 and Sunday 23:00 markers. Both the electricity price curve and the residual load curve drop during this period. The qualitative analysis evaluates the model with respect to the latter mechanisms and verifies whether the electricity market-specific correlations are discernible in the generated curves.

# 4.2. Quantitative analysis - deviation

To evaluate the performance of the probabilistic model, we used the Continuous Ranked Probability Score [48] $( C R P S )$ .

$$
\operatorname { C R P S } ( F , x ) = \int _ { - \infty } ^ { \infty } \left( F ( y ) - \mathbb { 1 } \{ y \geq x \} \right) ^ { 2 } d y
$$

The CRPS is a measure of the accuracy of a probabilistic model $F$ . With $F$ having a finite first moment, the $C R P S$ can be calculated as the negative Continuous Ranked Probability Score [49] (denoted by $C R P S ^ { * } )$ . The $C R P S ^ { * }$ is defined as follows:

$$
\mathrm { C R P S } ^ { \ast } ( F , x ) = \mathbb { E } _ { F } | X - x | - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \mathbb { E } _ { F } | X - X ^ { \prime } |
$$

![](images/d20fc489a67e7e9f0eb6fede7265fa34d729c117a8c76998b8a01401c5d9c278.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Characteristic day-ahead electricity price curve with low share of renewable energy.

![](images/9665f59a5165bae0506ef4f6db9014536594987f60858b15c2c9e2820ed59d97.jpg)  
Fig. 8. 5 Electricity price simulations.

$X$ and $X ^ { \prime }$ are independent draws of a random variable of a probabilistic model $F$ , while $x$ is the ground truth (the realized prices in the test period). The advantage of the $C R P S ^ { * }$ is that it can be expressed and interpreted in the same unit as the target variable itself. For deterministic models, the $C R P S ^ { * }$ is reduced to MAE (because $X$ and $X ^ { \prime }$ become identical the second term becomes zero), which makes this metric suitable for comparing probabilistic and deterministic models. This metric is commonly used in the evaluation of probabilistic forecasting models [50] as well as GAN models for time series [17,24].

We compare the performance in terms of evaluation metrics with different baseline models, including DeepAR [51], Prophet [52] and TFT [53], which are all capable of probabilistic time series modeling, while taking covariates into account.

# 4.2.1. Quantile ranges

A representative number (10,000 or more) of simulations can be used to determine quantile ranges for each hour of the day. Fig. 8 shows an example of 5 simulations for 22 February 2023. Since the quantiles refer to a specific hour and not to the entire curve, for example appending all $8 0 \%$ quantiles of each hour, results in a curve that does not necessarily correspond to a simulation. Rather, they indicate the hourly structure of how many of the simulated prices lie within a range. Theoretically, $6 0 \%$ of all simulated prices lie between the $2 0 \%$ quantile and the $8 0 \%$ quantile (not $6 0 \%$ of all simulated curves, as the quantile refers to the hour and not to the curve). This behavior is investigated empirically. A test data set is used to generate 10,000 simulations of unknown electricity price scenarios. We analyze the quantiles of the simulations according to a method proposed in [1] to evaluate factor models in the German electricity market. Quantile ranges are formed from the simulated scenarios. For example, exactly $2 0 \%$ of all realized values of the test data set are to be observed in the range, which is limited by the $0 \%$ quantile and the $2 0 \%$ quantile. The same applies to the range, the lower limit of which is given by the $2 0 \%$ quantile and the upper limit by the $4 0 \%$ quantile. Fig. 9 shows a heat map representing the theoretical best case. In the ideal case, the realized prices are evenly distributed across the quantile ranges, which were derived from the simulations.

# 4.2.2. Distribution of difference

Another important type of quantitative analysis in day-ahead electricity price modeling is the analysis of the distribution of the differences between simulated price and realized price in the test period, as proposed in [14]. A difference distribution is a probability distribution that indicates the probability of a certain difference occurring. We expect the mean of the difference to be zero and the standard deviation in a range to be typical for the electricity market. A skewed distribution can be an indication that the simulation tends to generate prices that are too high or too low.

# 4.3. Quantitative analysis - sensitivity

The inclusion of larger feature matrices as a condition to GANs has proven to be purposeful in many publications as showed in Section 2. The influence of individual features on the model output cannot be read from the model parameters. With the help of a sensitivity analysis, the model is tested for the variation of individual features. The aim is to evaluate the model in terms of its responsiveness to the characteristics of market mechanisms. The dynamics of the electricity market result from the influencing variables already discussed in Section 1. An increase in renewable energy generation raises downside risk and lowers upside risk. Factors such as changes in demand, available capacity from fossil fuels, and variations in price forecasts or previous day scenarios also impact the model’s price predictions. The sensitivity analysis is carried out under variation of the last mentioned features. The values of a feature are reduced or increased by 10, 20 and $3 0 \%$ respectively. Thus, 6 variations are tested per feature. For each variation, 10,000 simulations are generated. The change in average price indicates the sensitivity of the model to a feature. Fig. 10 summarizes the expected behavior of the model when the individual features are varied.

In practice an increase in renewable energy sources reduces residual load. For reasons of simplicity, no reduction in the residual load is carried out when increasing renewable energies. This imprecision can be accepted in order to be able to precisely determine the influence of the individual features.

# 5.1. Qualitative analysis

The simulations can be represented as intervals bounded by corresponding quantiles. The simulations can then be depicted as seen in Fig. 12. It shows the generated electricity price scenarios for Wednesday, July 5, 2023. The price range extends from approximately $- 5 0$ $\oplus / \mathrm { M W h }$ to $3 0 0 ~ { \in } / \mathrm { M W h }$ . When observing the red median curve, it is noticeable that the price rises in the morning and afternoon, whereas it follows a decreasing trend around noon and in the evening. This behavior was already illustrated and explained in Fig. 6 in Section 4. The simulated electricity price scenarios reflect this effect in the median and in the quantiles. Examining the range of the $9 8 \%$ interval (within which $9 8 \%$ of all generated prices fall), it becomes clear that the model exhibits high dispersion. Therefore, a mode collapse regarding the latent space vector can be ruled out for this example. Mode collapse describes an effect during the training of a GAN, which leads to the collapse of all generated outputs into one mode, no matter what noise is given as input. In the case of electricity price curves, this would mean that every simulation would look the same, regardless of the latent space vector. Mode collapse is a form of overfitting [54] and can be prevented, among other methods, by adjusting the learning rates of the generator and discriminator or by more frequent updates of the discriminator weights. Mode collapse in the case of electricity price curves can be qualitatively identified by plotting many curves given many different latent space vectors. If the curves show little variability or all look the same regardless of the input factors, it indicates mode collapse. Furthermore, a GAN converges at equilibrium when the discriminator loss is approximately 0.6 (see Section 3). Monitoring the discriminator loss as a function of training iterations can help determine the model’s convergence. The next step in the qualitative analysis is to consider the scenarios in light of forecasts and realized prices.

DeepAR, on the other hand, employs autoregressive RNNs to predict future values based on past observations and is often effective in handling large-scale time series, though it may struggle with the same level of complexity that TSS-CGAN can address.

As indicated by the previous results, it becomes clear that the model deems higher prices as more likely. An examination of the target variable can provide insight into this behavior. # 6. Conclusion

# 6.1. Strengths

As the results show, the simulated electricity price scenarios can be categorized qualitatively in the range of possible scenarios. The model reflects the influence of renewable energies on the market and behaves consistently with the seasonalities over the course of the day and week. The model’s ability to react correctly to specific market changes should also be emphasized, as the sensitivity analysis has shown. Taking the latent space vector into account, the model generates a wide range of possible price scenarios. This means that a mode collapse can be ruled out.

# 6.2. Weaknesses

The model systematically underestimates the price scenarios in the median for the early morning and late evening hours. The interval between the $8 0 \%$ and $1 0 0 \%$ quantiles should theoretically cover $2 0 \%$ of the values realized in the test period, but it is $9 \%$ . The tendency to overestimate of the prices also results in the skewed difference distribution and the frequent negative deviation of the generated prices from the realized prices. It is also still unclear how well the model will work if the input forecasts get worse. This offers scope for further research.

# 6.3. Outlook

In this work we showed how to construct a Conditional Generative Adversarial Network capable of generating electricity price scenarios. The TSS-CGAN can be trained on any electricity market and is also variable with respect to the features used (the features depend on the market and the actual availability to the user of the TSS-CGAN). One possible improvement is to use a Wasserstein loss with gradient penalty framework (WGAN-GP) [56] instead of binary crossentropy, as previous relevant researchers like Demir et al. [20] or Lu et al. [21] did. The WGAN approach could stabilize the training and improve the ability to generalize. Another way to achieve better results may be to increase the complexity of the model. For example, the TimeGAN approach by Yoon et al. [13] offers another way to generate time series generatively. This architecture has already been used in other work on forecasting energy market-specific time series, for example dayahead electricity price forecasting [22], synthetic consumption data generation [57] or PV power forecasting [58]. Generative time series simulation is a young field of research and is expected to find more attention in the coming years.
