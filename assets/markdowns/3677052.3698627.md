# Abstract

Predicting bankruptcy is crucial for managing financial risk in corporations.This study emphasizes incorporating the auditor's opinion text into prediction models to improve their ability to assess financial health.These opinions provide essential insights as they offer an independent assessment,complementing other predictive inputs like the management's discussion and analysis. However, the rarity of bankruptcy cases in the data introduces a challenging issue due to severe class imbalance.To address this, we propose a method to generate synthetic positive samples using a variational autoencoder and integrate the multi-source input in a late fusion setting.We showcase that both data augmentation and using multiple textual sources improve the performance of existing models on a related benchmark dataset.Additionally,we evaluate LLMs when used for data augmentation in the proposed method and in a zero-shot prediction setting, discussing important aspects to consider when incorporating them in a predictive pipeline.

# 1 Introduction

Bankruptcy prediction is a critical problem in financial research, driven by the need to foresee and mitigate insolvencies within corporations.It constitutes an essential part of the auditing process, where the auditor should express,among other things,their opinion regarding the company's future operation.Early work in the field proposed bankruptcy prediction models [2,3] to help auditors judge companies' abilities to continue as a going concern,i.e.,to continue their normal operation. Since then,many statistical and machine learning approaches have been proposed for the task of bankruptcy prediction using numerical variables as input [35,39] or text from the financial reports that reflect the management's opinion on the company [21, 29].

However, the complexity and multidimensional nature of financial health require more comprehensive data sources and advanced analytical methods.There is much evidence that the auditor's opinion (AO) text,which is included in financial reports,provides valuable insights into the financial standing and potential risks that each company faces, making it a potential source of input to bankruptcy prediction models [1o].A significant milestone was the Statements in Auditing Standards (SAS) No.59.Before that,auditors had a passive role in evaluating the going concern ability of a company. However, the release of the standard significantly increased the auditors'responsibilities by requiring auditors to take an active role in evaluating a firm's ability to continue its normal operation.

In the last three decades, therefore,the AO offers an independent and expert assessment of the company's financial practices and potential risks,which can highlight issues not evident in the MD&A section alone.These opinions can uncover underlying problems such as poor management practices,legal issues,or operational inefficiencies.By integrating these insights,models can achieve higher accuracy and reliability in predicting bankruptcies,leading to better risk management and decision-making. Utilizing multiple data sources allows for a more holistic view of a company's financial health,capturing nuances that the management outlook might miss or understate [1, 21].This is a direction that remains largely unexplored.

Moreover, the rarity of bankruptcy events introduces a significant challenge due to the extreme class imbalance in the historical data used for training algorithms.While most efforts rely on rebalancing,oversampling,or undersampling techniques [6],this study addresses the problematic issue of class imbalance through data augmentation,utilizing Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) [2o] and Large Language Models (LLMs). These methods generate synthetic data to better represent the minority class (i.e.,bankruptcy events), improving model training and prediction accuracy. By proposing a data-augmented and multi-source model that combines the management'sand auditor's opinion texts,thisresearchaims to enhance the robustness and reliability of bankruptcy predictions.

LLMs are wel-regarded for generating realistic data in augmentation tasks and are also becoming more common for interpreting financial documents,extracting information,and aiding decisionmaking.Therefore,it is unsurprising that such models may be used directly as predictors ina zero-shot classification setting[17].In the context of this work,we also use LLMs directly for the bankruptcy prediction task.We assess their ability to perform zero-shot classification and examine whether these models can infer the probability of bankruptcy, given an input text,or have overfitted on entities like specifc companies and people,which were seen during their training phase.We also highlight the high variance of the LLM responses when we alter the query prompt and evaluate whether these models can also be used in data augmentation.

The basis of this work is the recently proposed ECL dataset [5], which includes textual information from the company's management discussion and analysis (MD&A),accompanied by a label indicating whether the company filed for bankruptcy the following year.To support our intuition,we enrich this dataset with the AO text, introducing the multi-source aspect mentioned above to validate the proposed approach.In summary, this work contributes the following:

(1)We enrich the ECL dataset with the AO text,which is included in the (annual) financial report.We make it publicly available1 along with a demo² of our model, to facilitate and accelerate research in the field.   
(2) We propose a method for next year's bankruptcy prediction that exploits multiple sources as input and is based on data augmentation to address the rarity of the bankruptcy events in the data.   
(3) We perform multiple comparative experiments to assess the effectiveness of the proposed method and analyze the impact of the individual components used.   
(4) We experiment with LLMs in a zero-shot setting and discuss the challenges that should be considered when performing

a retrospective evaluation,as well as the high fluctuations in the performance when changing the query prompt.

The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related approaches in bankruptcy prediction and data augmentation; Section 3 details the proposed method's workflow; Section 4 covers the experimental setup and results;and Section 5 concludes with future directions.

# 2 Related work

# 2.1 Bankruptcy prediction research

Much work in the field has emerged from the pioneering work of Altman [4] that focuses on financial variables for corporate bankruptcy prediction and the work of Beaver [8] that focuses on the significant differences in financial predictors for bankrupt and non-bankrupt firms. Since then,many statistical and machine learning approaches have been suggested for the task, from traditional methods,such as logistic regression [16],to stacked autoencoders [30],aiming at the same time to expand the financial variables used to predict the bankruptcy events [14,38]. Seeking to improve the predictive performance of the trained algorithms,another line of work includes additional input variables,such as market data [22],company-related characteristics that reflect governance and personnel aspects [22],and country-level data [18].

On the other hand,there is significant evidence that textual parts of the annual reports of the companies can be good predictors in bankruptcy identification [26,28],and they are often combined with numerical variables [29,31].This body of work treats text either as a bag of words,relying on traditional weighting schemes to produce feature vectors,or employs deep neural networks to treat texts as sequences of words to capture the most relevant features to the prediction task.

A common characteristic is that the methods that use text input to predict bankruptcy events rely on a specific section of the annual report of the company that describes the MD&A section, and they examine whether the language used in financial filings reflects a firm's risk of bankruptcy [24].However, the aforementioned efforts consider different data samples from different periods and in different evaluation settings,making it hard to compare results across approaches.Aiming to address this situation, the recent work in [5] introduced the ECL dataset, that complements the management opinion section with financial variables from COM$\mathrm { P U S T A T ^ { 3 } }$ , providing at the same time an evaluation setting for next-year bankruptcyprediction.

Although this is a good starting point,the use of financial variables from proprietary vendors does not allow easy access to the full data. This means that the textual parts of the dataset are publicly available,but the financial variables are not.However,other parts of the annual report may help predict bankruptcy events.The work in [1o] provides evidence that the auditor's opinion,which is also included in the financial report of the company,may indicate issues that could correlate with bankruptcy cases.This is also supported by the work in [37],which examined financially distressed companies in the UK and found that the auditor's opinion is significantly associated with the severity of following financial distress outcomes.The authors also report that other textual parts, like the MD&A section,are primarily arbitrary and unhelpful to the users.Moreover,another study[32] concludes that the disclosures included in the audit reportand disclosures related to a firm's going concern status contribute the most to the prediction of financial distress.

In this work, we first extend the ECL dataset,found in [5], by enriching the samples with the auditor's opinion text,to facilitate research in the field.We also propose a multi-source method that takes as input both the MD&A and the auditor's opinion text,showcasing its improved performance over single-source textual models. Additionally,we also demonstrate that a late fusion of the features of these two sources outperforms early fusion techniques found in the literature.

# 2.2Data augmentation and LLMs

One of the main challenges in the task of bankruptcy prediction, as with many others in the financial domain,is the rarity of the bankruptcycases,i.e.,the positive examples in the data.This has resulted in many efforts to treat the learned classifiers as rankers [5,7,41] and evaluate accordingly by seeking the optimal threshold that maximizes the recall without sacrificing the precision much.However, this process alone does not address the issue of the extreme class imbalance and the difficulty of discriminating the positive from the negative examples.

Much work relies on rebalancing techniques through subsampling of the negative class (i.e.,healthy companies),which usually results in small sample sizes due to the small proportion of bankrupt companies [22,28],while others experiment with slightly imbalanced data, having a positive-to-negative ratio of 1:2 or 1:3[11].Regarding positive class augmentation, oversampling methods,such as SMOTE,are mainly used [3o]. Undersampling,oversampling, and other techniques are investigated in [6].

Data augmentation is one possible solution to the limited size and variability of the financial datasets.In this direction,Generative AIand LLMs can be used to generate synthetic data that can be used for training machine learning algorithms to increase robustness despite the original limitations of the dataset [34].Recent efforts have used LLMs for data augmentation in financial use cases to produce realistic data in financial markets [33] and create richer and more diverse datasets [15].In a similar attempt to increase diversity, the authors in [9],use LLMs to create additional positive pairs to the original data samples and perform a contrastive pretraining step that helps the model to learn a more discriminative space.

Besides data augmentation,LLMs are gaining attention in the financial domain since they can be used in several financial predictive scenarios,including insolvency forecasting and detection of financial distress signs [42].By analyzing the financial filings of companies,LLMs can reveal subtle linguistic or disclosure patterns and identify signs of financial distress,such as declining sales and increasing debt levels,that may indicate a risk of bankruptcy [19]. Moreover, the few-shot and zero-shot capabilities of LLMs allow for their direct application in the financial domain,where data may be scarce, without the need for extensive dataset-specific training. Zero-shot performance of LLMs in the financial domain has been assessed in stock movement prediction [40],stock market returns [25],time series forecasting[17],and others.

In this work,we propose that data augmentation is essential for addressing severe class imbalance in bankruptcy prediction. Using VAEs, we approximate the distribution of positive cases,leveraging the VAEs' ability to generate more representative positive samples than SMOTE or naive oversampling,which is the usual approach. Additionally,we experiment with LLMs by using them for data augmentation,as an alternative to VAEs,and ina zero-shot classification setting,following recent trends in the field.We highlight important aspects of incorporatingLLMs ina predictive pipeline, specifically focusing on the look-ahead bias in financial backtesting.

# 3Proposed method

The next year's bankruptcyprediction task of the ECL benchmark [5],presents certain challenges.First, the MD&A texts are too long,containing predefined general content in the introduction and conclusion. These sections include titles,general introductory sentences,and summaries essential for context and summarizing the main points.Second, the proportion of bankrupt cases to non-bankrupt cases in the training and test sets is extremely low.Bankruptcy events are infrequent, which makes it challenging for machine learning models to capture any relevant linguistic patterns.Third,the MD&A section serves as a narrative complement to the financial statements,focusing on explanations rather than numerical data.

Unlike the rigid standards of generally accepted accounting principles $\mathrm { ( G A A P ^ { 4 } ) }$ ,which govern financial statements,the MD&A provides flexibility in how the company presents its performance and prospects. Since this information is publicly accessible,management must balance transparency with strategic discretion to safeguard competitive advantages, which can result in misleading interpretations.

In this work,we address the challenges mentioned above in the bankruptcy prediction task.As highlighted in[5],there is a clear need fora complementary source of information to the MD&A section.The authors used numerical data from COMPUSTAT in addition to the management section,and their multimodal model performed better compared to its single-source counterpart.However, COMPUSTATisa proprietary database,and the related data are not publicly available.As an alternative,we use the AO,which is included in the annual reports.The AO indicates whether the financial statements present an accurate and fair view of the company's financial position and operations.Investors and regulators depend on this opinion to gauge the reliability of financial information and make informed decisions about the company. Together with the MD&A,these two sources reflect the opinions of two independent perspectives that may offer complementary information, benefiting the model.

As shown in Figure 1,we begin with the management and auditor texts for each company and create the corresponding tf-idf vectors.Although the AO texts are significantly shorter compared to the MD&A section,as shown in Figure 2,the length of MD&A texts makes the application of transformer-based approaches impractical. Therefore,regarding the text representation, we rely on 30K-dimensional uni- and bi-grams tf-idf weighted vectors.

In the next step,we deal with the crucial issue of avoiding overfitting the models on the majority class,i.e., the non-bankrupt cases. To this end, we deploy a data augmentation technique using a generative model to balance the proportions of the two classes. Specifically,we approximate the distribution of the bankrupt cases using a Variational Autoencoder. The VAE consists of an encoder $q _ { \phi } ( z | x )$ ,which maps input data $x$ to a latent space $z$ ,approximating the true posterior distribution $p ( z | x )$ ,and a decoder $p _ { \theta } ( x | z )$ which reconstructs the input data $x$ from the latent representation $z$ .By leveraging VAEs,we learn a latent representation space that captures the underlying structure of the bankrupt cases.We then sample from this latent space and use the decoder network to generate diverse and realistic synthetic samples of positive cases for both types of input text. These newly generated data points are then incorporated into our training dataset.With the augmented data, we train two separate models,each with either the management or auditor texts as input. These two models are Logistic Regression (LR) classifiers that take as input the corresponding tf-idf vectors and estimate the probability of bankruptcy. Then,at test time we combine these two independent predictions by simply averaging their probabilities.In Section 4.2,we discuss additional methods for fusing the two sources and evaluate each component in isolation as well.In Section 4.3 we explore the usage of LLMs as an alternative data augmentation technique,as well as their ability to perform predictions directly.

# 4.1 Experimental setup

As mentioned earlier, we base our analysis on the ECL dataset [5]. The ECL dataset includes the MD&A section and a binary label indicating whether the company filed for bankruptcy in the year following the report's fling date.The dataset consists of 84, 652 data samples,with 662 bankruptcy (positive) cases.It is provided with pre-determined training/test splits, with the training data ranging from1994 to 2015 and the test data from 2016 to 2021.

For our multi-source setting,we expanded the ECL dataset with the text of the AO.Specifically,we utilized an NLP tool (EDGARCORPUS [27]) to extract the AO text where possible (usually found in item8of the financial report)and align it with the existing flings in the ECL.Whenever the AO text extraction was unsuccessful, or the specific filing could not be found (especially for filings before the year 2ooo),we dropped the sample from the dataset.The statistics of the original dataset and the expanded dataset are shown in Table 1.

# 4.3 LLM-based experiments

Novel opportunities for machine learning applications in the financial domain have emerged due to recent advances in LLMs.Textual analysis in financial data,forecasting,predictions,and data augmentation are some prominent examples in the field [34].In this section, we incorporate LLMs in our work to experiment with zero-shot classification and data augmentation to emphasize several distinctive aspects of the field and outline challenges and opportunities.

4.3.1Zero-shot classification. We experiment with zero-shot classification using LLMs and discuss important aspects that should be taken into consideration when following such an approach. In the context of these experiments,we use the latest Llama-3 model [36] to predict directly in the test set whether there is a bankruptcy risk for a particular company. The main reason for focusing on Llama-3 is that it is the latest open-source LLM that has generic knowledge,and it has been trained on fifteen trillion tokens of data up to December 2023.For this purpose,we experiment with different input prompts to see how the results vary when re-phrasing the input.For completeness,we also experiment with a recent, financebased, fine-tuned Llama-2 model named Finance-chat [13].This 7B parameter model was pre-trained on a finance-specific corpus under a reading comprehension technique, showcasing competitive performance with much larger finance-specifc models like BloombergGPT-50B.

Each prompt asks the LLM to predict whether the company is at risk of bankruptcy and provide a probability score for the risk, using the AO as input.We choose the AO because,first, its length is small enough for the LLM to consume it without the need for summarization or text truncate approaches,and second, there is evidence that the auditor can indicate important issues regarding the financial health of the company that may correlate to the risk of bankruptcy, as evident in [1o] and in our experiments in Table 2,where the AO model alone can provide sufficient information related to the task. We experiment with a variety of prompts and include four of them (Table 4) that produced the best results in terms of F1 score.Each prompt has an introductory phrase,followed by the text of the AO.

An important aspect that should be taken into account is that the test dataset consists of historical data up to 2021.Recall that Llama-3 has been trained on data up to 2023.It is reasonable,therefore,to assume that during the pre-training process,the LLM may have encountered texts,news items,or other data sources that indicate the correct bankruptcy label for each company. Since the AO contains specific tokens about the company, such as its name, or other named entities,it is probable that the LLMwill not focus on the given prompt but rather rely on its prior knowledge to predict on the specific company.To avoid this situation in such a retrospective evaluation scenario,we also experiment with the same four prompts,but instead of having the original AO in the input, we use a named-entity recognition step to replace company names, person names,and other named-entities with a standard token,aiming to force the LLM to focus solely on the input text.

# 5Conclusions

We combined the auditor's opinion with the MD&A text to enhance bankruptcy prediction models,addressing the complex nature of financial health assessment.To tackle the extreme class imbalance, we used VAEs and LLMs to generate realistic synthetic data, improving minority class representation.This approach,combined with a late fusion technique,offered a more comprehensive view of financial health and outperformed single-source models. Our research indicates the auditor's opinion value in bankruptcy prediction and highlights the sensitivity of LLMs to the prompts used,their limited zero-shot performance in this domain,and the need to consider look-ahead bias.Future work could refine data augmentation, explore additional sources,and address the challenges observed in applying LLMs to boost prediction models'effectiveness.
