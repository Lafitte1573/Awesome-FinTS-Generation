# Summary

Financial statement fraud is a global problem for investors,audit firms, regulators, and other stakeholders. Fraud detection can be regarded as a binary classification problem with a false negative being more expensive than a false positive. Although existing studies have made great efforts to detect fraud using various data-mining techniques, the difference in misclassification costs is seldom considered. In this study, we propose a cost-sensitive cascade forest (CSCF) for fraud detection, which places heavy penalty on false negative prediction and self-adjusts the depth of a cascade forest according to the classifier's recall(i.e. the classifier's sensitivity). As missing values are ubiquitous in fraud research, we also explore the effect of selected missing data treatments on prediction performance, including complete case analysis, three selected classic statistical mechanisms (zero, mean,and modified mean imputation),and two machine learning (K-nearest neighbor [KNN] and random forest [RF]) approaches. The experimental results show that the proposed CSCF significantly improves the fraud prediction in comparison with one of the latest fraud detection models using the RUSBoost algorithm. Comparing different missing value treatments, even though RUSBoost and CSCF perform well when using complete case analysis, we find that the best performance is achieved when CSCF is used with missing data imputed as zero. Such treatment further improves the performance,and results in an area under curve (AUC) score of O.82 compared to the highest AUC (0.71) from the baseline model. Supplementary analysis further reveals that the low AUC of complete case analysis for the two examined models persists under different training sizes. Thus, our findings shed light on the potential benefits of missing value imputation for the model's performance for fraud detection.

# 1|INTRODUCTION

Financial statement fraud occurs when a firm deliberately alters its financial statement reports to be more attractive or to evade taxes. Unlike errors,financial statement fraud is usually intentionally hidden (Sithipolvanichgul,2O21).Therefore,it is extremely difficult to detect, evenfor professionals such as auditors (Earley，2O15；Warren et al.,2O15).The Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (AFCE), the world's largest anti-fraud organization,conducted a global analysis of occupational fraud over the period January 2O18 to September 2019. In their subsequent report, the total damage from fraud was found to be over $\$ 3.6$ billion, with $1 5 \%$ of the fraud cases being uncovered by internal auditors and only $4 \%$ by external auditors (AFCE,2020).To improve financial fraud detection, the Statement of Auditing Standards (SAS) No.99 (Auditors’Responsibility for Fraud Detection) requires auditors to take more proactive approaches in their audit procedures.

Data mining，which aims at extracting and uncovering hidden information from large quantities of data,hasadvanced classification and predictive ability.With the rising popularity and availability of data mining techniques,accounting firms have explored the applications of these methods in decision support systems to provide early warningsignalsforfinancialstatementfraud(Hajek& Henriques, 2017).

Prior research has examined the utility of various data-mining techniques to assist auditors in the task of fraud detection (Ngai et al.,2011;West & Bhattacharya,2016).These methods include decision trees (Kotsiantis et al.,2OO6),support vector machines (SVM) (Cecchini etal., 201O), neural networks (e.g. Fanning& Cogger,1998; Lin et al., 2015),evolutionary algorithms (e.g. Alden et al.,2012; Hoogs et al., 2OO7),ensemble learning models (e.g. Bao et al.,2020; Hajek & Henriques， 2017)，and text mining (e.g. Goel & Gangolly，2012; Goel & Uzuner,2016；Throckmorton et al.,2015). Although several studies have proposed that fraud detection models be built using both financial and linguistic or textual data (e.g. Craja et al.,2020; Hajek & Henriques,2017), the findings as to whether linguistic or textual data provide incremental information are inconsistent and subject to the linguistic or textual data selected.However, existing studies using financial data have consistently shown that financial data provide useful information for fraud detection,especially when used as input to an advanced data-mining approach.

The objective of this study is to developa new fraud detection model to predict financial statement fraud in listed US firms.Extended from the deep forest (DF) approach,an ensemble learning algorithm proposed by Zhou and Feng (2O19),we propose a cost-sensitive fraud detection model that considers the different costs of misclassification in fraud investigation. Ensemble learning methods are state-of-the-art machine learning algorithms used in several recent fraud-related studies (e.g. Bao et al., 202O; Hajek & Henriques,2017). However, the representation learning of traditional ensemble learning models uses only the original model features.DF has a deep structure similar to a deep neural network (DNN) model,where each layer consists of multiple decision-tree forests.The layer-by-layer processing enables in-model feature transformation in DF and, thus,increases model complexity, which theoretically may improve model capacity for complicated tasks.DF has had successful applications for problems such as cashout fraud (Zhang et al.，2O19)，drug-target interactions (Chu et al.,2021),and price prediction (Ma et al., 202O). In this study,we improve DF by adjusting it to accommodate the data imbalance problem and use it as our fraud detection method.The experimental results show that our proposed fraud detection model consistently outperforms the baseline model,and the performance is further enhanced by imputing missing data with zeroes.

# 2|LITERATUREREVIEW

The development of a financial fraud detection model is challenging due to the rarity of fraud observations.Aside from choosing the underlying learning algorithm,research on financial statement fraud prediction using data analytics has at least three decisions to make to build a fraud prediction model:(1) deciding which fraud predictors (input attributes) to use (ratios vs raw data),(2) deciding how to handle the embedded extreme class imbalance problem between the fraudulent and non-fraudulent observations in the dataset (sampling vs weighting),and (3) dealing with missing data.

We review past studies on these decisions in the following subsections.

# 2.1|Input atribute decisions: Ratios versus raw data

Forinputattributeselection，earlyrepresentativestudies (e.g.Beneish,1997,1999) calculated the multivariate score with expert-identified financial statement variables for earnings manipulation prediction.Dechow etal.(2011) and Perols (2011) documented a comprehensive set of financial statement fraud predictors in the published literature.These fraud predictors are often in the form of financial ratios and identified by human experts based on theory grounded in the earnings management or corporate governance literature (Perols et al., 2017).

Rather than constraining the potential fraud predictors to a certain format,several researchers use raw financial data decomposed from the fraud predictors as the inputs of their proposed models (e.g. Bao et al., 202O; Cecchini et al., 201O). By taking advantage of advanced data-mining techniques，prediction models that utilize dynamic combinations of raw financial variables can also identify fraud effectively.Cecchini et al.(2O1O) used a support vector machine applying a nonlinear mapping between raw financial variables via a kernel function to detect financial statement fraud.The prediction model of Bao et al. (202O)is based ona data-mining method called RUSBoost.Their best result is achieved by using raw financial variables,rather than the traditional financial ratios,suggesting the selection of variable transformations (i.e.raw variables vs ratios) may be dependent on the data-mining method used.

# 2.2|Class imbalance decision: Sampling versus weighting

Sampling methods are widely used in financial fraud research to handle the extreme class imbalance problem in fraud datasets.In particular, the dominant strategy is an undersampling one where fraudulent observations are matched with one or more non-fraudulent examples with certain criteria (e.g. Craja et al., 2O2O;Ravisankar et al.,2011). However,data preprocessing with such a matching strategy does not mirror reality and suffers from limited generalization because repeatedly identifying appropriate matching records may be difficult. By using an extensive database,some researchers turn to alternative resampling strategies to enhance the generalization ability. Usinga logistic regression,Dechow et al. (2O11) propose the F-Score for fraud detection and verify their model with all non-financial US publicly traded firms.Perols (2011) generates a more balanced training sample by randomly selecting non-fraud observations and compares multiple algorithms for financial fraud detection.Extended from Perols (2O11), Perols et al. (2017) propose to address the imbalance issue in fraud datasets with multisubset observation undersampling (OU),where subsets of non-fraud samples are randomly collected for the training model with varying subsample sizes.They also systematically evaluate another two undersampling strategies and show that OU offers robust improvement to the prediction performance.Ina more recent study, Bao etal.(2O2O) report a state-of-the-art result for detecting fraud in US-listed firms by applying RUSBoost,an ensemble model characterized by an in-built random sampling function on the majority class, similar to OU.

However,sampling is not the only approach to handle the class imbalance problem: cost-sensitive weighting is an alternative.In the data-mining literature,cost-sensitive learning (Elkan,2OO1) is presented as a viable alternative to sampling approaches and is frequently used in research efforts in imbalanced learning scenarios (fora review,see Galaretal.,2O11; He& Garcia,2OO9). Rather than balancing data distributions through various sampling approaches,cost-sensitive learning biases the classifier toward the minority class assuming higher misclassification costs for the minority class.For certain application domains, various empirical studies have shown that cost-sensitive learning techniques outperform sampling ones (e.g. Liu & Zhou, 2OO6; Zhou & Liu, 2005).In the fraud literature,Moepya et al. (2O14) and Twala and Nelwamondo (2O17) apply cost-sensitive classification algorithms (e.g.weighted SVM,cost-sensitive Naive Bayesand K-nearest neighbor [KNN]) for financial fraud detection under high class-imbalance.Kim et al. (2O16) develop financial misstatement prediction models to identify misstatements with fraud intention.To deal with class imbalance and reflect asymmetric misclassification costs, the researchers employ MetaCost,a data preprocessing technique that relabels examples in the training dataset to minimize cost.In general,cost-sensitive classification forimbalanced learning has had limited application in the financial statement fraud literature.1

# 2.3|Dealing with missing data

Missing data are ubiquitous in fraud research.There is a large literature in data mining that has documented various approaches to deal with unknown values and their effect on the experimental results (e.g. Feelders,1999；Twala, 2009). Surprisingly, few existing fraud studies have systematically discussed this issue or have shown whether their experimental results are affected by the missing value treatment used.Within the same context,early studies such as Cecchini et al. (201O),Dechow et al. (2011),and Perols (2011）employed complete case analysis to handle missing data. This method simply excludes entire firm observations that have missing values.Other fraud studies handle missing values with single-value imputation using statistical techniques,such as mean and zero imputation,or machine learning techniques,such as support vector machines.For example,Perols et al. (2O17) preprocess the data by replacing the missing values with global means,for fraud prediction.Missing values are re-coded to zero in Walker (2020) and partially in Bao et al. (2O2O) before feeding into their proposed fraud detection models. Other research imputes the missing data by values estimated with available variables through various machine learning algorithms (e.g. Craja et al.,2O2O；Hajek& Henriques, 2017).

Complete case analysis,where only records with no missing values are retained for analysis,is a crude way to treat missing values.It may introduce bias and,more importantly,it omits some useful information from analysis. Statistical methods that substitute the missing values with the zero/mean/median may improve the performance depending on the context,such as the variation and the skewness of the data. Machine learning-based techniques are a more advanced way ofhandling missing data，where absent valuesareimputedbyestimatingthemwithavailable information (Batista & Monard, 2003；Howell, 2007). However, the benefits ofmissing data treatments aresensitive tothe problem domain，patterns，and size ofthe available dataset (Allison, 2001).

# 2.4丨Summary and discussion of prior research

To summarize,prior research has shown that the choice and format of fraud predictors is dependent on the prediction algorithm chosen to fully utilize its learning capacity and offer the best performance. Furthermore,even though undersampling with matched observations for class imbalance problems is common in the fraud literature,research with this strategy often fails to indicate performance on out-of-sample cases and has been criticized for its limited generalization ability.The use of an extensive dataset enables an out-ofsample test of the prediction model,in a more realistic way.However,research testing such an extensive dataset is lacking and needs to tackle the extreme imbalance class problem either by applying an appropriate algorithm or by applying an undersampling scheme.

The existing fraud literature also lacksa systematicanalysis of the treatments of ubiquitous missing values in data,most particularly in the case of cost-sensitive learning methods.

In the next section,we introduce a novel DF that incorporates both cost-sensitive learning and missing value imputation,and we propose and extensively evaluate our fraud detection model based on the modified DF.

# 3| THE PROPOSED FRAUD DETECTION MODEL

# 3.1| An introduction to deep forest and cascade forest

Deep Forest is a tree-based ensemble classifier recently proposed by Zhou and Feng (2019).The principle of “the wisdom of the crowd" suggests that the collective opinions of large groups of individuals can be better than that of a single expert.In machine learning,such crowd wisdom is achieved by ensemble methods where results from a set of base learnersare intelligentlyaggregated for betteraccuracy,generalization，and robustness (Zhou，2O12).Various ensemble learning methods have achieved notable success in real-world applications and offer impressive results in the fraud literature (e.g. Bao et al., 2020; Craja et al., 2020).

Inspired by the layer-to-layer processing in the deep neural model, the core part of DF is constructed as a multi-layer structure, known as the cascade forest (CF) structure.In the CF,each layer consists of several units called the base classifiers.Each base classifier is an ensemble of decision-tree forests.To enhance the diversity,different types of forests are employed.² The CF processes the information ina feed-forward manner. Input features are randomly assigned to the base learners at each layer.The first layer only processes the original features.For the rest of the layers,processed feature information from the preceding layers is concatenated with the original input features to form input features for the connected layer.Each layer is added on sequentially.K-fold cross-validation isapplied for prediction in each base classifier to reduce the risk of overfitting.After expandinga new layer,the algorithm calculates the performance gain,and the training procedure will be terminated when no significant gain is found. Hence,the optimal level of layers in the CF is adjusted automatically and is self-adapting.To apply the CF,one has only two hyperparameters to tune: the number of forests at each layerand the number of trees in each forest. Compared to the well-known deep neural networks,the CF has fewer hyperparameters to tune while offering competitive performance (Zhou & Feng,2019).Theoretically, methods whose performance depends on the configuration parameters tuned are less robust,as changes in the data might alter their results.Thus,with only two hyperparameters, the CF has the advantage of being robust and has good generalization ability.

# 3.2丨 Cost-sensitive cascade forest

To meet the need for fraud detection,we propose a variant of CF called the cost-sensitive cascade forest (CSCF),which is a cost-sensitive-based CF with self-adapting depth according to recall (sensitivity); that is,the percentage of total relevant results correctly classified by the classifier.For financial fraud,misclassification costs are asymmetric,where the cost of a Type Il(false negative) error (failing to identify fraudulent statements) is usually much higher than the cost of a Type I (false positive) error (misclassifying the non-fraudulent statements) (Hajek& Henriques,2017).

The idea of cost-sensitive learning can be naturally applied to the fraud prediction problem.To begin with,we construct the proposed CSCF with the same types of base classifiers as the original CF but placing a heavy penalty on misclassification of the minority class by assigninga larger weight to the minority class.Here,we follow the definition of a cost matrix using the class distribution,which is based on a cost-proportionate weighting of the training samples (Zadrozny et al.,2Oo3).A different cost matrix may influence the goal of costsensitive learning,which is to minimize the total cost of classification. Given that each decision tree in the forest is generated from a bootstrapped dataset (e.g.random selection with replacement),the class distribution in the sample is different for each tree.The balanced weight is applied in each bootstrap sample for every tree grown, instead of the entire training dataset.For simplicity,class weights are setto be inversely proportional to their respective frequencies in each bootstrap sample.' Thus,in the case of the two-class fraud detection problem,the calculation of the weight for class iin a subsample is as follows:

$$
w _ { i } = \frac { n _ { - } s a m p l e s } { n _ { - } c l a s s e s \times n _ { - } s a m p l e _ { i } } ,
$$

where $w _ { \mathrm { i } }$ refers to the weightforclassi;n_samples is the total number of observations in a bootstrap sample; n_classes is the number of classes,which is two for the binary fraud detection problem;n_samplei indicates the total number of observations of class i in that bootstrap sample.

We further modify the original CF by changing the depthterminating criterion from accuracy to recall (sensitivity).The difference in misclassification costs explains the preference for higher recall as the preferred performance metric (vs other performance metrics like accuracy and precision) in prior fraud detection studies (Craja etal.,202O). By resetting the layer growth stopping criteria, CSCF can further enhance the performance in a way that the growth of the cascading forest ends when there is no increase in recall score for the additional layer.The overall structure of our CSCF is illustrated in Appendix A.

Existing studies have identified various fraud indicators, including financial items from financial statement reports.While the majority of research employs financial ratios in their fraud prediction models, Bao etal.(2o2O) showed that the readilyavailable raw financial variables can be sufficient for fraud detection.We follow Bao's study and use only raw financial items and the same variable selection procedure. # 4.2丨 Sample selection procedure

As one of the most developed capital markets, the US market is a popular choice for financial fraud research.As our theory-motivated financial variables are generated from research based on the US market,we test the financial fraud detection models on publicly traded US firms. # 4.3丨Missing value treatments

Firm observations with missing values of Centravel Index Key(ClK) or missing，zero,or negative values for sales and total assets are dropped.Missing data in financial variablesare handled with complete case analysis and with five missing data imputation methods for comparison.Among them,three (zero,mean,and modified mean imputation) are statistical approaches,and the remaining two (KNN and RF imputation) are machine learning approaches.As our data is large, which makes the cost of deleting observations affordable,we keep firm-year observations with less than $2 5 \%$ of their values missing and insert the missing values with the five chosen methods.A heuristic cutoff line of $2 5 \%$ is used to ensure that we utilize the majority of information in the dataset.7 Global mean and zero missing data imputation are commonly used statistical techniques (Allison,2Oo1). However，as shown in Appendix B,the standard deviation of many variables is large.Replacement with global means may introduce biases.Instead,we treat each firm's time-series observations as an individual subset.Mean imputation is applied to each individual subset if the values of the variables are partially missingand re-coded to be zero when a variable in the subset has empty values.We refer to this in this study as modified mean imputation.For missing data imputationwith machine learning algorithms,we select KNN (Duda & Hart,1973) to represent the clustering approach and RF (Liaw & Wiener,20o2) to represent the tree-based approach.Both KNN and RF are widely used to test the effect of missing data imputation on machine learning algorithms (e.g.Purwar& Singh,2015).The sample selection and missing value treatment are presented in Table 1.

# 4.4Labeling

Fraudulent financial reports were identified using the SEC's Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Releases (AAERs).The AAERs describe the enforcement actions taken against an auditor,a company or an individual for violations of SEC and federal rules followingan investigation.These releases contain information about the entities and individuals involved,the nature of the misconduct,and the consequence of such misconduct for financial statements at varying degrees of detail.The AAERs are the prevailing source used to identify fraudulent firms in previous studies (e.g. Craja et al.，2O2O；Fanning $\&$ Cogger,1998;Feroz et al., 200O;Humpherys et al., 2011;Purda $\&$ Skillicorn,2O15).AAERs are suitable for misconduct research that has highrequirementsforaccurate fraud identification(Karpoff et al.,2017). Because the SEC only applies enforcement actions on important fraudulent activities with sufficient and robust evidence, AAERs are considered a simple,trustworthy and consistent source for identifying fraudulent firms.However,as investigations are timeconsuming and investigations may take months or years to complete, the AAER dataset may not cover the most recent periods of interest. The sample period: Consistent with Bao et al. # 5.2Evaluation metrics

K-fold cross-validation is commonly used to estimate the performance of models on new data. As noted in Section 5.1,the time necessary fora fraud investigation to reach a conclusion makes our fraud data intertemporal in nature.Therefore,we use data from more recent years as the test set for classification performance evaluation instead of $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { k } }$ -fold cross-validation.This study considers performance metrics, including the area under the curve (AUC) (Bradley,1997),normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG) (Järvelin $\&$ Kekälainen,2002), recall at the top $1 \%$ ，precision at the top $1 \%$ ,and F-score at the top $1 \%$

The AUC is our primary evaluation metric.It refers to the area under the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve,which is a probability set plotting the true positive rate (TPR)against the false positive rate (FPR).AUC captures a classifier's ability to distinguish between classes,and it is widely used in fraud detection literature. The higher the AUC,the better a model can distinguish between fraudulent and non-fraudulent observations.In the case of an imbalanced class distribution like fraud detection,AUC is preferred to accuracyas a high level of accuracy can be achieved bya naive guess of the majority class.

Fraud detection models can also be evaluated in terms of ranking of the predicted likelihood for fraud.Because the investigation of fraud is costly,instead of showinga full list of suspicious financial statements identified by the models,a more cost-efficient and practical way might be presenting the results in a shortened list from the top decile of risk according to the probability of fraud.Referring to the information retrieval literature,we select NDCG,recall@k,precision@k,and F-score@k as the measurement metrics to evaluate the model's prediction performance at the top of the probability distribution.The threshold is set to be at the top $1 \%$ of the probability scores because the average proportion of fraud in the observed years is under $1 \%$ ,as shown in Table 2.

As one of our primary metrics,NDCG is a popular measurement for ranking problems.The value of NDCG is higher when a true positive (true fraud) observation is scored higher than a negative observation,or when a true positive is ranked higher.14 The F1-score is calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall.Precision measures the percentage of correct predictions for the positive class, and recall (sensitivity) refers to the percentage of total relevant results correctly recognized by the classifier (i.e.,of all available fraudulent observations,what percentage the classifier recognized as fraudulent). As maximizing precision will reduce recall and vice versa,F1 score offers a balanced performance metric indicating the robustness and precision of the classifiers for distinguishing fraudulent observations. As shown in Hajek and Henriques (2O17), the cost of a Type Il (false negative) error (failure to identify fraudulent statements) is approximately twice as high as the cost of a Type I (false positive) error (misclassifying the non-fraudulent statements).An F2-score,a metric that assigns more weight to recall than to precision,is also employed.We will present the recall,precision,and F2-score using the top $1 \%$ of firms with the highest fraud probabilities as the second set of measurement metrics.

# 6丨 SUPPLEMENTALANALYSIS

# 6.1|Alternative fraud proxies and test period

As noted in Section 4.4, the material accounting misstatements disclosed in SEC's AAER are the dominant fraud proxy due to high confidence in indicating intentional misreporting (Dechow et al.,2011; Donelson etal.,2O21).Considering there isa downward shift in SEC enforcement of accounting fraud since the 2Oo8 financial crisis,in the prior section we ended our test period in 2Oo8 to avoid potentially increasing false negatives.

Otheraccounting fraud proxies are also used in financial misconduct research. Besides AAER,fraud labels can also be inferred using data from private enforcement via securities class actions (SCAs). Donelson et al. (2O21) review the relevant legal standards and processes.They show that settled SCAs can also be used as a valid proxy for fraud.Restatements are another source to identify fraud or financial “irregularities”in some misconduct research studies (e.g. Brown et al., 2020; Hobson et al., 2017).

The measurement errors are different for each fraud proxy (Amiram etal.,2018;Karpoff etal.,2017).As the SEC only targets firms when there is strong evidence of intent to mislead (Donelson et al.,2O21),a fraud proxy relying on AAER has a low false positive error. However,it also suffers from relatively high false negatives caused by the undetected accounting fraud problem (for a review, see Amiram et al.,2018;Karpoff et al.,2017),especially after the SEC shifted its investigation focus around 2Oo8.One way to partiallysolve this false negative problem is through supplementing AAERs with settled SCAs that violate General Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) (Donelson et al.,2021).The main concern for the studies using the restatements as a proxy for fraud is its false positive problem,as not all restatements are intentional financial misreporting (Karpoff et al.，2O17).Thus,a subset of restatements is usually used to reduce the false positive errors (e.g. Hobson et al., 2012, 2017).

In this section，to test how the prediction models perform under various levels of fraud measurement errors，we replicate ourbest results inPanelB ofTable 3(missing values imputation with zero) using longer test periods and two alternative fraud proxies.

First,we extend the test samples relying on AAER to examine the performance of models when the fraud proxy is more likely to be subject to false negative issue.Because there are onlya few fraud observations recorded in the AAER database for the years 2O15 and 2016, we present the experimental results until 2O14,as shown in Table 5. Second, the work of Donelson et al.(2021) shows the benefit of using a combination of misconduct databases for accounting fraud research. We construct one of the alternative fraud proxies by overlapping the settled ${ \mathsf { S C A s } } ^ { 1 6 }$ with fraudulent restatements flagged in Audit Analytics $( \mathsf { A A } ) ^ { 1 7 }$ and keyword searching of “GAAP” and “General Accepted Accounting Principle” for setled SCAs with GAAP violations.18 All identified SCAs were reviewed for additional fraud indicators.This procedure generated 52 fraudulent firms with 126 fraudulent firm years besides the ones identified based on AAER.As settled SCAs with GAAP violations can be a valid alternative fraud proxy, the additional fraud indicators may mitigate the false negative problem when fraud identifications are solely based on AAER,while keeping relatively low false positives.

The development of the third fraud proxy follows several existing studies where a subgroup of restatements are used to proxy fraud (e.g. Brown et al.,202O; Hennes et al., 2008; Karpoff et al.,2017).The identifications of intentional misreporting (fraud) are based on flags in AA:(i) fraud is flagged if the restatement disclosure references financial fraud,irregularities,or misrepresentations;and (ii) fraud is also flagged if the restatement is neither an error in accountingand clerical applications nor a misapplication of a rule.As most SEC investigations do not lead to formal public enforcement (Solomon& Soltes,2021), we exclude the SEC investigation flags to mitigate the false positives. While combining AA with AAER may reduce the false negatives,it may also induce false positives because fraud flags in AA may involve fraud committed by lower-level staff seeking personal gain.The resulting distributions of fraud observations using the three alternative fraud proxies are shown in Table 4.

The experimental results using the three fraud proxies are presented in Table 5.As the cases for credible fraud allegations decline over the years,19 the measurement errors for each fraud measure may become larger,which leads to a decrease in performance of both models in general. However, the proposed CSCF consistently outperforms the baseline model in each test year no matter which fraud measure is used.More importantly, it is clear that RUSBoot fails (low AUC,close to O.5) for recent years,but CSCF is resilient throughout the 15-year period of analysis from 2003 to 2018,with AUCs consistently in the satisfactory O.6-O.8 range and significantly higher than the AUC cutoff of O.5 for models that are no better than random guessing.

Comparing NDCG scores,to evaluate each model's performance in fraud prediction as a ranking task, CSCF outperforms RUSBoost. NDCG is an appropriate metric for the fraud detection task considering the low frequency of fraud and the SEC's constrained investigation resources. Bao et al.(2O2O) notice that RUSBoost and learning algorithms used in other studies (e.g. Cecchini et al.,2O1O) tend to overestimate the number of actual cases of fraud.In contrast,we observe that CSCF tends to underestimate it.20 However,this could make our proposed fraud detection model more valuable considering fraud investigation is expensive.Although the value of AUC using AA combined with AAER as the fraud proxy is the lowest in all test periods,the value of NDCG is competitive,suggesting it might be a good fraud proxy from a practical perspective,especially when little or no AAER data isavailable.

# 6.4丨 The effect of more financial variables

Bao et al. (2O2O) raised the question as to whether more data helps to improve the model's performance.By applying RUSBoost toa full set ofraw financial variables (266 additional raw data items)，the researchers refute this hypothesis. Because the researchers implemented the algorithm without imposing any missing value restrictions, the results might change if missing valuesare treated differently.As argued in Section 4.3,RUSBoost in Matlab handles the missing data with the mechanism of a decision tree.In other words,the algorithm only searches for patterns in the given data for prediction and thus biases the data.The more missing values occur in the data,the less likely an algorithm can achieve its best performance.As shown in many studies in the data-mining literature,statistical imputation or a supervised learning imputation may be superior methods compared to surrogate splits for addressing the missing values in tree-based learning.This effect is more profound when the training data contains many missing values (Saar-Tsechansky & Provost,2OO7；Bhekisipho Twala,2009). Here,we offer an alternative analysis of the question.

Tore-examine the question,we downloaded all financial items from Compustat's “Fundamentals Annual’ database.21 After excluding variables with more than $2 5 \%$ of values missing,our final list of variables contains 187 financial items.A simple zero replacement for the missing values is used to illustrate the importance of missing data treatment.Table 7 presents the experimental results for the test period 2O03-2008.Panel A reports the results using Matlab's RUSBoost,which has no requirement for preprocessing for missing data. Panels B and C report results using Python with zero imputation for the missing values.

From Table7,experimental results verify our concern about the outcome induced by the in-built splitting mechanism in Matlab's RUSBoost algorithm.Compared with the same algorithm imputing missing data with zero,the results of Panel Aare much worse,which leads to a doubtful conclusion that including additional financial variables does not help to improve the fraud prediction.When zero imputation is used,Panels B and C reveal that the performance of both models is improved,while our proposed CSCF outperforms RUSBoost,consistent with our previous findings.

However,additional features are often costly to gather and computationally expensive for model training.As reduced features and equivalent performance are more cost-effective to execute in practice,feature selection has become crucial in areas of application for which datasets with many variablesareavailable.We conducted the feature selection based on the importance scores of 187 financial variablesand divided the dataset into several subgroups for performance and computational time comparison.We selected the top 28,50, 100,and 15O features as well as full features and report the average AUC of 2003-2005 and the average time per layer in Figure 3.22

Figure 3 confirms the common observation in data mining that additional features are more computationally expensive as the time used for computing each layer increases with the number of features. However,more features may induce noise and do not always result in higher AUC,suggesting the importance of feature selection for the development of a model. More importantly, the AUC from our main experiments (O.82) is higher than the AUC of the top 28 features subset (O.79)and is close to the AUC using the top 5O features.This finding indicates that theoretical guidance is still important for the model's feature selection,especially for developinga computationally cost-effective fraud detection model.

# 7 CONCLUSION

This study examined whether an improved financial fraud detection tool could be developed through the application of a recently introduced machine learning algorithm (deep forests/cascading forests).The dataset consisted of fraudulent financial statements identified using AAERs,accountingand auditing enforcement releases,and full samples of publicly traded US firms from the years 1991 to 2008,with supplemental analysis using alternative fraud proxies (SCA and AA) for more recent years (up to and including 2O18).A new out-of-sample fraud prediction model based on cost-sensitive learning was developed and compared with the one recently proposed by Bao et al. (2O2O).We distinguish our study from the benchmark work by the novel learning algorithm we employ，the alternative missing value treatments we used,and the comprehensive supplemental analyses we performed using additional fraud proxies (SCA and AA) and more recent data years. Samples with no more than $2 5 \%$ of their values missing were kept.The remaining missing values were treated with complete case analysis and imputation with three statistical (zero,mean,and modified mean imputation) and two commonly used machine learning (KNN and RFimputation) approaches.The experimental results show a notable performance improvement by our proposed CSCF detection model compared to the benchmark.This enhanced performance is robust across different test periods,various missing data treatments,different training sizes,and when using alternative fraud proxies.In addition, the results regarding the effect of missing data treatments indicate that our imputation method significantly improved the proposed model's performance compared to complete case analysis.

# 7.1丨Implications for research

First,our research shows that cost-sensitive modeling is effective for classification in domains with imbalanced data such as fraud detection where misclassification costs vary greatly between classes.Our model and results can be used asa baseline for cost-sensitive classification for future studies.Second,our research implies researchers should consider the effect of various missing data treatments on the performance of fraud detection models. How missing data should be handled seems to be neglected in the fraud literature.Our research reveals that an appropriate missing data treatment may enhance financial fraud detection.Thus,future researchers are advised to explore or conduct robustness tests on different missing data treatments.

# 7.2丨Implications for practice

Considering the prevalence of missing values in real-world applications and the practical need fora fraud detection model among stakeholders,in this study,we propose a CSCF enhanced with missing data imputation that can be used as an effective tool to generate complementary evidence foraccounting fraud detection purposes.Byanalyzing the feature importance in Section 6.3,we show that the most significant predictors of our proposed model have a substantial overlap with the account categories most frequently influenced by the misstatements.This suggests our proposed model may also be used to identify specific account categories that are most likely to be affected by the misstatements.

# 7.3Limitations

The main limitation of this study is the imperfect fraud proxy measures.As discussed in Section 6.1,each fraud proxy has its own limitations (e.g. the limited time period of the proxy measure and the limited scope of the proxy measure regarding the types of accounting misstatements).Because the performance of fraud detection models differs among fraud proxies,a fraud proxy that truly represents its underlying construct has become an impediment to the fraud detection task,yet it offers opportunities for future research.Another limitation is that we only employ structured data available in public financial statements for the fraud detection model. Other structured or unstructured data (e.g.employee messages) may also be informative for the prediction of fraud.

# 7.4Future work

A natural extension of this work includes evaluating the benefits of alternative missing data treatments.In addition,for simplicity，we applied a simple inverse-proportions-based cost matrix for costsensitive learning.The cost matrix can potentially be adjusted and optimized for better learning.Finally,our robustness analysis reveals the importance of theoretical guidance for feature selection when designing a fraud detection model.It points to a potential research direction for future researchers to developa theory-motivated feature selection for more effective fraud detection models.
