# Abstract

Predicting financial time series presents significant challenges due to inherent low signal-to-noise ratios and intricate temporal patterns. Traditional machine learning models exhibit limitations in this forecasting task constrained by their restricted model capacity. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs), with their greatly expanded parameter spaces, demonstrate promising potential for modeling complex dependencies in temporal sequences. However, existing LLM-based approaches typically focus on fixedlength patch analysis due to the Transformer architecture, ignoring market data’s multi-scale pattern characteristics. In this study, we propose ??????4??????, a novel framework that enhances LLM capabilities for temporal sequence modeling through learnable patch segmentation and dynamic wavelet convolution modules. Specifically,we first employ K-means $^ { + + }$ clustering based on DTW distance to identify scale-invariant patterns in market data. Building upon pattern recognition results, we introduce adaptive patch segmentation that partitions temporal sequences while preserving maximal pattern integrity. To accommodate time-varying frequency characteristics, we devise a dynamic wavelet convolution module that emulates discrete wavelet transformation with enhanced flexibility in capturing time-frequency features. These three modules work together to improve large language model’s ability to handle scaleinvariant patterns in financial time series. Extensive experiments on real-world financial datasets substantiate the framework’s efficacy, demonstrating superior performance in capturing complex market patterns and achieving state-of-the-art results in stock return prediction. The successful deployment in practical trading systems confirms its real-world applicability, representing a significant advancement in LLM applications for financial forecasting.

# 1 Introduction

Financial time series prediction has long been regarded as an extremely challenging task in quantitative analysis, primarily due to the inherently weak predictive signals obscured by massive market noise and non-stationary temporal dynamics[15]. These challenges are aggravated by complex macro-economic interdependency[11], irregular event-driven disturbance, and the heterogeneous behavior of market participants[10], which collectively manifest as intricate patterns that resist conventional market analysis methods. As illustrated in Figure 1, the visual comparison between financial data and power data reveals that the intrinsic patterns in financial time series are more complex and exhibit similar patterns across different scales. Moreover, the frequency domain characteristics of financial time series vary over time, which contrasts sharply with power time series. All of the above highlights the low signal-to-noise ratio and complex dependencies characteristic of financial time series.

Traditional machine learning methods, including auto-regressive models[30] gradient-boosted decision trees[23] and recurrent neural networks[13], face limitations when applied to financial prediction tasks. These limitations stem from their restricted model capacity to simultaneously capture long-term sequence dependency, multi-resolution features, and nonlinear interactions among diverse market factors. Furthermore, the inherent assumption of stationarity in many traditional models fails to account for the non-stationary nature of financial time series, where statistical properties such as volatility and correlation structures evolve over time. For instance, while recurrent neural networks are capable of modeling sequences, their practical efficacy is undermined by gradient vanishing issues and inflexible receptive fields when processing extended financial sequences spanning multiple market regimes. This architectural rigidity prevents such models from adapting to the ever-evolving statistical property of financial markets.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have introduced transformative opportunity for time series modeling, leveraging tremendous parameter scales exceeding hundreds of billions to establish precise representation of complex dependency[4, 26, 31]. The transformer architecture’s self-attention mechanism[32], originally developed for language processing, showing remarkable potential in capturing long-term sequence dependency through its capability to model pairwise interaction between arbitrary time steps[24, 37, 45]. Primary application of LLMs in financial domain has shown promising results in sentiment-driven price prediction, suggesting their latent capacity to decode structured temporal patterns in market data[14, 40, 41].

However, current LLM-based financial prediction methods exhibit critical flaws that limit their practical utility. For instance, Time-LLM [16] employs textual information to reprogram time series, concatenating output prompts with reprogrammed patches to enrich the context. However, when dealing with complex financial time series, the modal information within each patch is challenging to accurately characterize through simple textual prototypes, and the integration of time series inputs using prompts appears excessively redundant. Most existing methods rely on fixedlength temporal patch segmentation, arbitrarily dividing time series into uniform intervals without regard for the intricate multi-scale nature of market reality[3, 5, 48]. This uniform segmentation inevitably breaks semantically coherent patterns, thereby discarding critical contextual information. Furthermore, the static nature of conventional patch decomposition fails to accommodate the timevarying frequency characteristics of financial data, where different frequency components dynamically influence price movements.

Recognizing the potential of LLMs in financial time series modeling and the limitations of existing approaches, we propose the LLM4FTS framework to fully realize the potential of LLMs for financial time series prediction. Our framework integrates an offline scale-invariant pattern recognition algorithm, a learnable patch segmentation strategy, and a dynamic wavelet convolution module to enhance the modeling of financial time series data by capturing its multi-scale characteristics and complex temporal dependencies. Specifically, we first conduct an offline study based on sequence pattern scale invariance, optimizing patch lengths using clustering and dynamic time warping (DTW) algorithms to preserve complete sequence patterns. We then employ a two-phase self-supervised training strategy: the first phase involves causal training on financial time series datasets from diverse markets to align LLM capabilities with the data’s complexity, while the second phase finetunes the model for multi-resolution prediction in target markets. A key innovation is the dynamic wavelet convolution module, which adaptively updates wavelet transform basis functions during training, enabling dynamic time-frequency analysis at multiple scales. By replacing the traditional wavelet transform with an adaptive parameter matrix convolution, our framework significantly improves the applicability of wavelet transforms in model training.

In summary, the primary contributions of this work include:

• We propose the LLM4FTS framework. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to enhance large language models to model the complex interactions between patches while capturing the scale-invariant patterns and temporal dependencies inherent in financial time series. • We devise scale-invariant pattern recognition algorithm, learnable patch segmentation strategy, and dynamic wavelet convolution module. These methodologies are designed to collectively enhancing the capability of large language models to effectively model financial time series data. • We conducted extensive experiments on four real-world datasets, and the results demonstrate that LLM4FTS outperforms state-of-the-art baselines. Ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of our proposed methods. We successfully deployed our approach in a real-world trading system, achieving consistent excess returns over a year.

# 2 Methodology

# 2.1 Problem Formulation

The definition of the stock forecasting problem can vary depending on the specific investment strategy adopted by the investor. In this paper, we adopt a paradigm widely accepted in the research field, namely cross-sectional analysis[2, 12]. This approach involves analyzing relevant data at a given moment in time in order to predict the future returns of all stocks at a given point in time. Accurate forecasts are expected to provide investors with stable profit opportunities. Building on existing work, we input historical data on normalized stocks with multiple metrics (e.g., stock opening price, closing price, or volume) and output a predict of the next day’s return. Given a set of $N$ stocks $X = X _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } , . . . , X _ { N }$ consisting of all data from the stock market, each stock $X _ { i } \in R ^ { T \times F }$ contains historical data with a backtracking window length $T$ , where $F$ denotes the indicator dimension at one time step. Our task is to predict the return $r _ { i } ^ { t }$ on trading day $t$ . Denoting our model parameters as $\Theta$ , the process can be expressed as follows:

$$
{ X \in R ^ { N \times T \times F } \overset { \Theta } { \longrightarrow } } r \in R ^ { N \times 1 }
$$

In this paper, we focus on predicting the stock’s return the next day. Therefore, the label of the predicted value r is defined as $r _ { t } ^ { s } =$ $p _ { t + 1 } ^ { s } / p _ { t } ^ { s } - 1$ , where $\mathbf { \nabla } _ { P _ { t } ^ { s } }$ denotes the closing price of the stock.

# 2.2 Scale-Invariant Pattern Recognition

We suggest a way to spot patterns in financial data based on the idea that financial time series have a scale-invariance nature. Using the K-means clustering algorithm and the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance metric algorithm, the whole financial time series is split into segments of different lengths and grouped into K different clusters. After scaling the segments so that their duration and magnitude are the same, segments within the same cluster have similar shapes. The idea is like traditional K-Means clustering, which mostly puts similar parts of things together in the same groups. But this doesn’t work for our task because we need to be able to handle parts of different lengths. Instead of using the same length for all parts of the time series, like most people do, we figure out the best length for each part using a simple but effective way of splitting things up. Following the normalization of the sequence, the process entails the iteration through all potential segment lengths. For the candidate length $l \in \{ l _ { m i n } , l _ { m a x } \}$ at position $\begin{array} { r } { t = \sum _ { \tau = 0 } ^ { m - 1 } t _ { \tau } } \end{array}$ of the sequence, we obtain it by minimizing the distance between the subsequence and the cluster centroids pattern for each possible length, while $x _ { m } = X _ { t : t + l ^ { * } }$ is considered as the optimal segmentation:

where $m$ is the number of sequences that have been segmented and $\mathbf { p }$ is the cluster centroid sequence used for the comparison.

To address the need for calculating distances between segments of varying lengths and to focus on the differences between various modes, we adopt the DTW distance measure in place of the standard Euclidean distance method. DTW is particularly effective in calculating the minimal distance between all aligned points of two segments, rendering it highly appropriate for our purpose of identifying similarities in segments that possess similar shapes but vary in duration and magnitude.

Consider $X = \{ x _ { 1 } , x _ { 2 } , \ldots , x _ { N } \}$ and $Y = \{ y _ { 1 } , y _ { 2 } , . . . , y _ { M } \}$ , where $N$ and $M$ denote the lengths of sequences $X$ and $Y$ , respectively. The DTW algorithm computes the minimum cumulative distance between these sequences, allowing for non-linear alignments. The cumulative distance $D _ { i , j }$ is defined as:

$$
D _ { i , j } = d ( x _ { i } , y _ { j } ) + \operatorname* { m i n } ( D _ { i - 1 , j } , D _ { i , j - 1 } , D _ { i - 1 , j - 1 } )
$$

where $d ( x _ { i } , y _ { j } )$ is the local distance between points $x _ { i }$ and $y _ { j }$ , typically the Euclidean distance. The final DTW distance is $D _ { N , M }$ representing the optimal alignment cost between the sequences.

In the initialization of cluster centroids, we adopted the method used in FTS-Diffusion[15] to mitigate the adverse effects of random initialization on clustering results. Specifically, we first randomly select a segment of the predetermined length from all available segments. Then, from the remaining segments, we choose the one that is farthest from the already selected segment to ensure sufficient dissimilarity among the centroids. This process is repeated iteratively until K centroids are selected. Compared to the traditional approach of randomly initializing K centroids and iteratively updating them, this method demonstrates greater robustness when dealing with segments of varying shapes. Our algorithm is represented by Algorithm 1 in pseudocode. When partitioning the data, we employ the DTW algorithm to compute the distances between segments and seek to minimize the distances between the resulting sequences and the cluster centroids, to achieve an optimal segmentation method that preserves as much of the pattern information within each segment as possible.

The patch extraction process is dynamically determined based on both predefined segment positions and a sliding window strategy. Specifically, for each batch of data, we calculate the start and end indices of the current batch respectively. We then determine the positions of segments within this batch by adjusting the global segment positions stored to be relative to the start of the batch. These positions are combined with the stride-based starts, generated by stepping through the batch with a fixed stride, to form the complete set of starting points for patch extraction.

Let $S$ be the list of segment starts derived from the global segment positions adjusted for the current batch. We then take the union of these two sets of starts, sort them, and remove duplicates to obtain the final list of patch starting positions, denoted as $S _ { S }$ . For each start position $s \in S _ { S }$ , we extract a patch of length $\mathcal { P }$ from the tensor $\mathbf { x }$ if $s + L _ { p } \leq L$ . This results in a list of patches $\mathbf { p }$ , each of which is a slice of $\mathbf { x }$ of size $( B , M , L _ { p } )$ . We then stack these patches along a new dimension to form a tensor of shape $( B , M , N _ { P } , L _ { p } )$ . To prepare the data for the subsequent layers, we rearrange the dimensions of $\mathbf { x }$ to $\left( { B \times M } , { N _ { P } } ,  \tmspace + \thinmuskip { . } { p } \right)$ and concatenate it with the output of another input layer applied to y, resulting in the final input tensor of shape $\left( B \times M , N _ { P } , d _ { \mathrm { m o d e l } } \right)$ . This approach allows us to efficiently handle time series data by dynamically extracting relevant patches based on both predefined segment positions and a sliding window, ensuring that the model captures both specific segments of interest and the general temporal dynamics of the data.

# 2.4 Multi-Resolution Prediction Fine-tuning

To handle the input time series data ${ \bf X } _ { t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { N \times T \times D }$ from the stock market, we first rearrange it into the shape $( B , M , L )$ to align with the model’s input requirements. Here, $B$ represents the batch size (which is fixed at 1) multiplied by the number of stocks, $M$ is the number of features, and $L$ is the sequence length. The data from different stocks are treated as channel-independent variables, ensuring that the model processes each stock’s time series separately while maintaining the structural integrity of the input. Let x ∈ R??×??×?? denote the input tensor. The low-pass and high-pass filters of the Daubechies wavelet, $\mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ and $\mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ respectively, are used to initialize the weights of the convolution layers:

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { l o w } } = \left[ h _ { 0 } , h _ { 1 } , \ldots , h _ { N - 1 } \right] } \\ { \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } } = \left[ g _ { 0 } , g _ { 1 } , \ldots , g _ { N - 1 } \right] } \end{array}
$$

These filters are used to initialize the convolution layers’ weights:

$$
\mathbf { W } _ { \mathrm { l o w } } ^ { ( i ) } = \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { l o w } } , \quad \mathbf { W } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } } ^ { ( i ) } = \mathbf { h } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }
$$

where $\mathbf { W } _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ and $\mathbf { W } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ are the weights of the low-pass and highpass convolution filters, respectively, for the $i$ -th channel. The key convolution operations to perform the wavelet transform are defined as:

$$
\mathbf { x } _ { i } = \mathrm { C o n v 1 d } ( \mathbf { x } , \mathbf { W } _ { i } , \mathrm { s t r i d e } , \mathrm { p a d d i n g } ) , i = l o w , h i g h
$$

Here, $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ and $\mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ are the low-pass and high-pass filtered coefficients, respectively. The stride of 2 and padding of $\textstyle { \frac { N } { 2 } }$ ensure that the output length is halved while maintaining relevant signal information. The inverse wavelet transform combines the low-pass and high-pass coefficients to reconstruct the original signal. First, the coefficients are upsampled by inserting zeros between elements:

$$

$$

Next, these upsampled coefficients are convolved with the reconstruction filters:

$$
\mathbf { x } _ { i } ^ { r e c } = \mathrm { C o n v 1 d } ( \mathbf { x } _ { i } ^ { u p } , \mathbf { h } _ { i } ^ { r e c } , \mathrm { p a d d i n g } ) , i = l o w , h i g h
$$

These orthogonalized filters update the weights of the convolution layers, preserving the orthogonality of the filters and enhancing the model’s capability to analyze the financial time series.

In summary, by incorporating the Daubechies wavelets’ low-pass and high-pass filters into the convolutional layers and dynamically orthogonalizing them, we effectively capture the time-frequency characteristics of the stock market data, improving the model’s ability to process and analyze financial time series.

At the end, we give an implementation of the objective function. We model the stock trend forecasting task as a ranking forecasting problem. Specifically, we use the next day’s return of each stock as the forecasting objective and optimize the LLM4FTS using a combination of point-by-point regression and pairwise rankedperceived loss to reduce the discrepancy between predicted and actual returns while maintaining the relative order of the top stocks with higher theoretical investment returns:

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { d _ { \boldsymbol { \phi } } ( i , j ) = p _ { i } - p _ { j } , d _ { \boldsymbol { r } } ( i , j ) = r _ { i } - r _ { j } } \\ & { \mathcal { L } ( i , j ) = \mathrm { R e L U } ( ( M - d _ { \boldsymbol { \phi } } ( i , j ) ) ^ { 2 } [ d _ { \boldsymbol { \phi } } ( i , j ) * d _ { \boldsymbol { r } } ( i , j ) < 0 ] ) } \\ & { \mathcal { L } = \alpha \displaystyle \sum \mathcal { L } ( i , j ) + \beta \| r _ { t + 1 } - p _ { t + 1 } \| ^ { 2 } } \end{array}

where $\mathbf { \nabla } \mathcal { P } i$ and $r _ { i }$ respectively denotes the prediction return ratio and ground-truth return ratio of stock $i$ on every trading day, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are hyper parameters used to control for different loss ratios.

# 3 Experiments

# 3.1 Experimental Setup

3.1.1 Dataset. We conduct our experiments using data from the Chinese and US stock markets. These datasets include: CSI $3 0 0 ^ { 1 }$ , CSI $5 0 0 ^ { 2 }$ , S&P $5 0 0 ^ { 3 }$ , and NASDAQ $1 0 0 ^ { 4 }$ . For all these four stock datasets, we use metrics such as the opening price, closing price, high price, low price, trading volume, and turnover. To ensure consistent data presentation and mitigate the effects of outliers, we perform outlier removal and normalization of stock price data. We calculate the daily returns for each stock, defined as the percentage difference between the day’s closing price and the previous day’s, and rank the daily returns to be used as labels for the dataset. We divide the data chronologically into a training set (2018-2022), a validation set (2023), and a test set (2024) to avoid data leakage problems from interfering with the results of the experiment.

3.1.2 Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate the experimental results using six key financial metrics, including: Annualized Rate of Return (ARR) evaluates the overall returns of a stock investment strategy by aggregating the daily returns of selected stocks over one year. Annualized Volatility (AVoL) measures the risk of an investment strategy, calculated as $\mathrm { A V o l } = \sigma \times \sqrt { 2 5 2 }$ , where $\sigma$ represents the standard deviation of log returns. Maximum Drawdown (MDD) captures the maximum decline from peak to trough within a specific time period, reflecting potential loss risk. Annualized Sharpe Ratio (ASR) evaluates performance adjusted for risk, calculated as $\begin{array} { r } { A S R = \frac { A R R } { A V o L } } \end{array}$ . Calmar Ratio (CR) assesses the relationship between returns and maximum drawdown, calculated as $\begin{array} { r } { C R = \frac { A R R } { | M D D | } } \end{array}$ . Finally, the Information Ratio (IR) quantifies the ratio of excess returns to additional risk. Comparing these metrics comprehensively helps evaluate the performance and risk of investment strategy. We aim for higher ARR, ASR, CR and IR values, while considering lower AVoL and MDD values. Multiple repeated tests are conducted to obtain average performance, ensuring robustness in results.

# 3.3 Ablation Study

We conducted comprehensive ablation experiments to evaluate the impact of removing each sub-module on model performance across four datasets, and detailed results are presented in Table 2. Our strategy consists of three main aspects: an offline learning module for pattern matching, a two-stage pre-training strategy for prediction, and a dynamic wavelet transform module. It can be observed that using the original GPT2 module resulted in a significant performance decrease, indicating that the two-stage pre-training strategy plays a crucial role in enhancing the model’s performance. Furthermore, adding any of the sub-modules on top of this strategy improves prediction accuracy, demonstrating that both modules contribute to enhancing the model’s performance. Specifically, the offline learning module effectively captures long-term dependencies in financial data, while the dynamic wavelet transform module enhances the model’s ability to extract multi-scale features. When employing the two-stage pre-training strategy with all modules present, our model exhibits the best performance, further confirming the importance of strategy and modules in our model when utilizing LLMs for stock price prediction. Our research underscores the importance of maintaining the structural and informational integrity of financial data and emphasizes the significance of customizing LLM for financial time series prediction.

Figure 4 presents the parameter sensitivity results of our proposed LLM4FTS method across four market datasets. The first row examines the impact of GPT layer depth. # 4 Related Works

# 4.1 Methods For Financial Prediction

Traditional statistical machine learning methods, such as Exponential Smoothing[7], ARIMA[1], have been widely used in financial prediction due to their ability to capture linear trends. With the increase in financial trading data volume and the advance in computational power, machine learning methods such as Support Vector Machines[18], XGBoost[35], have become the main financial prediction methods. However, due to the high volatility and low signal-to-noise ratio of financial data, these methods are prone to overfit. Deep learning methods such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) efficiently model the sequential dependency of data and are prevalent in financial prediction tasks[8, 19]. But they exhibit poor performance in handling long-range dependency relationships. To address this, diffusion models originating from the image generation domain have been used to analyze and predict intricate patterns within financial data. FTS-Diffusion[15] is a framework aimed at synthesizing financial time series, capturing scale-invariant patterns in the data through three key modules. Aimed at modeling intricate dependency, Transformers have been widely used in time series prediction[37, 45, 47]. Transformers exploit multi-head self-attention mechanism to sequentially process input data in an auto-regressive fashion, thereby significantly reducing training time through parallel computation. They also avoid recursion by processing all inputs concurrently, enabling them to handle longer input sequences. However, the inherent low signal-to-noise ratio often leads to poor performance in generative models[43]. As a result, financial prediction models typically combine with other methods for noise reduction or information enhancement[25, 39, 43].

# 4.2 LLMs for Financial Prediction

As representatives of deep transformer models, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated their effectiveness in various domains, such as NLP[4, 26, 31] and visual language (VL)[46, 49]. LLMs can make use of the internal and external knowledge stored within the parameters, together with the contextual understanding ability, to achieve data enhancement[17, 29], thus opening up a new possibility to model financial time series.

Current LLMs-based financial time series prediction methods can be divided into two categories. The first category aims to train LLMs for financial forecasting tasks using market-driven feedback[34, 38, 41]. Open-LLaMA[41] uses its inherent knowledge to comprehend and reason through textual news and price time series information for financial prediction. Stocktime[34] regards the stock price as a sequence of tokens, directly extracting textual information such as stock correlations, statistical trends, and timestamps from these stock prices to predict the next token, effectively forecasting the stock price within any look-back period. However, these models typically rely on external information such as high-quality text message, this can be challenging to acquire. In such cases, FinLLM trained under these circumstance often leads to less-than-optimal result. Our method directly captures the inherent data features, effectively avoids the limitation of collecting textual information.

The second category of methods directly uses existing generalpurpose LLMs adapted for time series tasks for prediction. These methods include: generating appropriate prompts or reprogramming sequences based on input data to align time series features with language features[5, 16]; or pre-training and fine-tuning LLMs with large amounts of time series data to promote knowledge transfer across different domains using frozen pre-trained modules[3, 22, 48]. However, these methods rely on fixed patch length strategy, overlooking the inherent multi-scale complex patterns in financial data. Existing multi-scale time series prediction frameworks typically combine information from different frequency domains that have been captured[27]. Our method measures different scales in financial time series through patches of various lengths, naturally capturing the inherent scales and explicitly learning cross-scale dependency in data. This method avoid disruption of price correlation within the same inherent period in financial data, and significantly improving prediction accuracy.

# 5 Conclusion

This paper introduces LLM4FTS, a novel framework enhancing large language models for financial time series prediction. The proposed architecture integrates a scale-invariant pattern recognition module with an adaptive dynamic patch segmentation strategy, enhanced by trainable wavelet convolution operators for multiresolution temporal dependency modeling. This combination enables systematic analysis of complex historical patterns in financial data through hierarchical feature extraction. Comprehensive evaluations were conducted across four heterogeneous markets. Empirical results demonstrate the framework’s superior performance against state-of-the-art baselines in both forecasting accuracy and generalization capability. To assess real-world performance, we integrated the model into the production-grade algorithmic trading infrastructure of a securities exchange platform. Comparative analysis with existing investment strategies reveals significant improvements in cumulative returns, confirming the operational efficacy and economic viability of our approach under real-market conditions.

# A Implementation Details

# A.1 Infrastructure and Hyper-Parameters

In the implementation, we use a unified data input approach and backtesting framework for most of the baseline methods with the parameter settings recommended in the original paper. For the time series forecasting model, we use a Top-P strategy to maintain the portfolio for each trading window, selecting the top $1 0 \%$ of the highest rated stocks as positions at each update. On each trading window we use the historical trading data of the last 31 days as input to predict the returns for 1 day in the future. For our approach, we set the GPT layer to 6, the dropout to 0.2, the learning ratio to 0.0002, and the feedforward layer dimension to 128. we use the PyTorch framework and python as the backend. Our model is experimented on a server with 251G RAM and 4 NVIDIA Telsa V100 GPUs.

# B Extra Experimental Results

# B.1 Investigation With Financial Time Series

Inspired by tokenizer design in the NLP domain, we posit that financial time series exhibit variable durations of pattern repetitions, where morphologically similar patterns may recur at different temporal scales. This scale invariance property, initially identified by FTS-Diffusion in financial time series analysis, remains underexplored in relation to the prevalent patch segmentation strategy within contemporary temporal sequence analysis. We argue that current fixed-length patch segmentation approaches are suboptimal, as they impede effective learning of scale-invariant patterns in sequences. As demonstrated in Figure 6, our comparative analysis of subsequence patterns generated by dynamic versus fixed-length segmentation reveals critical truncation artifacts in fixed-length segmentation outputs (highlighted regions). The proposed dynamic segmentation method, implemented through DTW distance-based K-means $^ { + + }$ clustering, demonstrates superior capability in capturing complete pattern structures and trend information.

In Figure 7, we show the collection of cluster-centred patterns obtained on each dataset. Similar simple patterns such as unilateral up, unilateral down, etc. are obtained on each dataset, but there are also variations among different datasets. Upon comparative observation, we find that the cluster centred patterns in the two US indices are relatively simpler and homogeneous, with fewer complex patterns such as double or triple bottoms. On the other hand, the cluster patterns of the two Chinese indices are more complex, with inverse head-and-shoulder and triple-top patterns. This may be attributed to the differences in market rules and fundamental trends between the two markets in recent years.

# B.2 Pilot Experiment and Case Study

Based on the aLLM4TS codebase, we investigate the distribution of attention coefficients in LLMs for time series forecast. Attention scores reflect the similarity between query and key vectors, and attention weights result from applying softmax to these scores. We hypothesize that time series data sets with strong periodic characteristic also exhibit periodic features in their attention distributions.

To demonstrate this hypothesis, we extract and visualize the attention weight matrices generated by all GPT blocks. The exported attention weight matrices have a shape of (layern, samplen, headn, qlen, klen), where layern represents the number of layers in the model, samplen is the quantity of input samples at a particular layer, headn denotes the number of attention heads, qlen signifies the length of input sequence for query involvement in attention score computation, and klen indicates the length of input sequence for key involvement in attention score computation. By selecting the first three dimensions as parameters, we obtain specific Q-K positional matrices by truncating the attention weight matrices and visualize them in heatmap form to reflect the relationship between attention scores and the positions of key or query vectors.

Firstly, we select the number of layers and attention heads, iterating through the samples’ indices to generate the corresponding heatmaps. This visual representation vividly illustrates the distribution of attention weights, enabling to observe whether a particular attention head exhibits concentrated attention patterns towards specific key or query positions. Brighter regions on the heatmap signify areas where the model is focused more intently. Experiments are conducted on two datasets, namely the electricity and ETTh1 datasets known for strong periodicity, and the CSI 300 stock dataset. In the case of the CSI 300 dataset, experiments are performed sequentially on the fourth and fifth stocks to analyze the attention patterns. Through the comparison of results, we can observe that the regularity of attention weight distribution in the electricity and ETTh1 datasets is significantly stronger than that in the CSI 300 dataset, which corroborates our hypothesis. Across all three datasets, there is a notable concentration of high weights along the diagonal in the attention matrices. This indicates that the model exhibits a strong self-dependence on the time series data, as each time point tends to focus more on its own and neighboring positions. The heatmap corresponding to the CSI 300 data lacks globally high-weighted regions, which means a lack of long-term periodic structural patterns in the data, with more emphasis on local and short-term autocorrelation. Furthermore, the irregular patchy distribution of weights in the CSI 300 data’s heatmap suggests a random pattern that aligns with the oscillatory nature of stock data, reflecting discontinuities in the model’s attention along the time axis. Given the inherently complex nature of price behaviors in the stock market, the absence of evenly spaced periodic distributions in the heatmap signifies the non-uniform cyclic patterns typically seen in stock data.

Lastly, we investigate the evolution of attention weights for a specific sample across different layers. We begin by selecting the target layers for observation and then establish a loop iterating through the layers. Within the loop, we average the weights across attention heads for the chosen sample at each layer and create corresponding heatmaps to visually depict the distribution of attention weights. This cross-layer analysis aids in understanding the hierarchical structure of the model, wherein early layers focus on local features and periodic patterns, while later layers emphasize global information. With the model comprising six layers, we choose to visualize layers 1, 3, and 6. Experiments are conducted on the electricity and ETTh1 datasets known for strong periodicity, as well as the CSI 300 stock dataset.

By conducting cross-layer comparisons, we can find that the concentrated area of attention remains near the diagonal in the third layer, but regions of high weight exhibit some contraction. Particularly towards the top portion, there is increased density and higher weight values in the high-weight regions, indicating a focus on data relationship within shorter distances in this layer. Although periodic diagonal lines persist, they are noticeably weaker compared to the first layer. This suggests that as the layers deepen, attention gradually shifts from capturing periodic features to a more localized dependence and short-term fluctuations. In the sixth layer, attention primarily focuses on the upper left part of the diagonal, with further reduced weight values, indicating a heightened concentration on dependencies within very short periods. There is minimal recurrence of periodic features, and the model’s attention towards distant data diminishes progressively. In all layers of the CSI 300 dataset, distinctive periodic diagonal lines as seen in the electricity dataset are absent, implying a lack of global periodic structures in the stock dataset. As the number of layers increases, the high-weight regions near the diagonal in the attention weights become sparser, weights become more dispersed. The randomness and short-term volatility characteristic of the stock market are particularly evident in these attention maps.
