# A B S T R A C T

Stable and accurate carbon price forecasts are crucial for carbon asset management and policy design. Therefore, various advanced models have been proposed to forecast carbon price, and decomposition–reconstruction models occupy a major position. On this basis, this study proposes an improved decomposition–reconstruction framework and constructs a novel hybrid forecasting model Informer-DA-BOHB-TVFEMD-CL. The hybrid model decomposes carbon price by TVFEMD to obtain different volatility characteristics and classifies these features by time series clustering, where the high-frequency components are retained and the low-frequency components are reconstructed. Then, the training set was expanded by the generative data augment technique to improve the model’s generalization ability and avoid overfitting. Finally, the advanced deep learning model Informer and the hyperparameter optimization technique BOHB are used to model the training set, and the prediction results of each subcomponent are integrated to obtain the final prediction results. Experiments are conducted in EU-ETS, Guangdong, Hubei, and Chinese national carbon market. Experimental results show that the stability and prediction performance of the proposed hybrid model are better than those of the benchmark models, proving the applicability of the improved decomposition–reconstruction idea and the validity of the hybrid model. Meanwhile, the proposed model has demonstrated good profitability in carbon trading.

# 1. Introduction

Global climate change has become the biggest environmental problem facing humanity in the 21st century. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report [1] shows that the global surface temperature in 2011–2020 has risen by ${ } ^ { 1 . 1 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } }$ from 1850 to 1900 levels. A rapid and deep low-carbon technology transition is now needed globally to meet the Paris Agreement target of limiting this century’s warming to ${ \bf 2 } ^ { \mathrm { ~ \tiny ~ \circ ~ } } { \bf C } ,$ , and the deployment of low-carbon technologies requires strong policy support. Carbon markets, as a key policy tool for combating climate change, are derived from the Kyoto Protocol and play an important role in global carbon emissions reductions [2]. Carbon markets internalize the externalities of emissions through market-based tools, thereby inducing high-emission enterprises to reduce emissions and thus pursue a green transition [3]. Carbon markets have been established in many countries and regions, and the European Union established the world’s first and largest existing carbon market, the European Union Emissions Trading System (EU-ETS) in 2005 [4]. Regions such as California and Quebec have also established regional carbon markets. As the world’s largest emitter and the country with the fastest-growing energy demand [5], China established eight carbon trading pilot markets in 2013, and the national carbon market has been officially launched in 2021. In the future, the Chinese carbon market is likely to replace the EU-ETS to become the world’s largest carbon market in coverage. Carbon price, as a core driver of the carbon market’s role in reducing emissions and promoting energy transition, is critical to building an efficient and sustainable market.

The fluctuations in carbon price have a direct impact on firms’ emission reduction strategies. Accurate carbon price forecasts can provide a decision-making basis for enterprises’ low-carbon investments [6], as well as a reference for enterprises and policymakers to understand the fluctuation patterns of carbon price [7], thereby accelerating the realization of emission reduction targets. Therefore, carbon price forecasting has attracted considerable attention from researchers in recent years. Compared with traditional financial markets, carbon markets are still not sufficiently well-established with higher uncertainty, higher volatility, and non-stationarity embedded in the series, all of which pose a challenge to accurate carbon price forecasts. Traditional models such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH), and grey forecasting have shown considerable predictive power in forecasting regular series such as energy production [8] and demand [9], carbon emissions [10], and so on. However, for financial data with high frequency fluctuations they were usually used for volatility analysis rather than forecasting. Zhang et al. [11] analyze the asymmetric volatility of Chinese seven carbon pilot markets using the Exponential GARCH (E-GARCH) model, with the volatility mainly concentrated in the early stage of the market’s establishment as well as in the annual compliance phase. Dhamiija et al. [12] predict the volatility of EU-ETS carbon quota price based on the ARIMA-GARCH model and find that the GARCH model better captures the volatility in carbon price. These traditional models have strict stationarity and normal distribution assumptions and fail to capture nonlinear features in carbon price [13,14]. These reasons limit traditional statistical methods. To solve these problems, machine learning-based and especially deep learning-based models have shown superior performance in time series data processing. In different fields such as engineering [15], pollution control [16], and financial markets [17] have demonstrated the robustness and ability of deep learning to handle nonlinear features. For carbon price forecasting, many studies have demonstrated the deep learning model’s higher forecasting accuracy compared with traditional statistical models. Li et al. [18] used a multidimensional long short-term memory (LSTM) model to predict the carbon price in China, and the experimental results show that the multidimensional LSTM outperforms multilayer perceptron (MLP), support vector regression (SVR), and recurrent neural network (RNN) in terms of MAE, MSE, and RMSE. Zhang and Wen [19] constructed a temporal convolutional network (TCN) architecture based on sequence-to-sequence to predict complex carbon price, and TCN prediction results outperform traditional statistical models and LSTM by multidimensional comparisons. However, each model has its own shortcomings, and the performance of a single model is often limited [20]. In particular, the superposition and chaotic character lead to the complex fluctuation characteristics in carbon price, and processing these complex fluctuating features can also further improve the performance of deep learning.

Hybrid models, which have now become the mainstream carbon price forecasting models, are mainly based on the decomposition–reconstruction framework, where the decomposition algorithm decomposes the complex and non-stationary carbon price series into several smoother subsequences, which are usually called intrinsic mode functions(imfs). Each imf contains different temporal characteristics and similar fluctuation characteristics in the same imf. These imfs are forecast individually, and the prediction results are then aggregated. This reduces the model’s complexity in the learning of the original series [21] and improves model accuracy. Commonly used decomposition algorithms include empirical mode decomposition (EMD), wavelet transform (WT), and variational modal decomposition (VMD). Liu and Shen [22] combine GRU with WT, WT decomposes the carbon price into several more stable and regular subsequences, contributing to the performance of GRU. Chai et al. [23] use a hybrid prediction model based on VMD and extreme learning machine (EL) with eight of China’s carbon trading pilot markets as the experimental subjects, and the experimental results proved that VMD promoted the accuracy of EL, which was significantly better than the single benchmark model. Zhu et al. [24] verified that EMD can improve the accuracy of carbon price prediction, and the use of EMD can effectively reduce the non-stationarity, non-linearity, and complexity of carbon price. EMD leads to poor decomposition results due to its sequence aliasing and endpoint distortion issues [25]. Many scholars have therefore begun to use improved EMD methods. Zhou et al. Yun et al., and Wang et al. [26–28] all use CEEMDAN as the decomposition method and LSTM as the prediction model, and the experimental results show that the decomposition effect of CEEMDAN is better than EMD and EEMD. These studies have shown that decomposition method significantly improves the prediction performance, and a suitable decomposition method needs to be chosen. With further usage of decomposition methods, different ways to process imfs emerged. Some researchers have argued that too many imfs could contain a lot of redundant information that would lead to error accumulation and excessive computational costs and therefore merged redundant imfs using sequence reconstruction. Wang et al. Li et al., Yang et al. Jiang et al.[29–32], they used an entropy-based approach to compute the complexity of different imfs obtained by CEEMDAN and merge the imfs by entropy value. By contrast, other researchers believe that high-frequency imfs contain more complex features and that using only single decomposition is not sufficient, so secondary decomposition of high-frequency imfs used to further improve accuracy. Li and Liu [33] used the discrete wavelet transform to secondary decompose the high-frequency imfs obtained from the improved CEEMDAN. Sun and Huang [34,35] performed a second decomposition for high-frequency imfs by VMD based on a single decomposition of EMD. The two approaches can be found conflicting, with one merging the imfs to simplify the computation and the other generating more imfs through secondary decomposition. Therefore some researchers have combined these two ideas by first merging the imfs obtained from the first decomposition and then performing a secondary decomposition of the merged high-frequency component[26,36–38]. This process further refines the high-frequency features of the carbon price and merges the redundant imfs to improve the prediction accuracy. But some problems arise with these processing methods in the merging of different imfs. Previous research adopts an entropy-based approach to classify imfs, this classification is done manually to discriminate the threshold value, which is subjective and lacks a unified discriminatory standard. In addition, the idea of secondary decomposition is due to the number of imfs obtained in the first decomposition being relatively less. At the same time, the characteristics of carbon price fluctuations tend to be complex, and the different volatility characteristics in carbon price are not fully captured. This leads to a secondary decomposition to obtain more imfs. Based on these two points, this study draws on the idea of time series clustering to categorize imfs by a clustering method [39], which avoids the subjectivity of imf division. Time-varying filtering for empirical mode decomposition (TVFEMD) is also adopted as the decomposition method. TVFEMD eliminates the mode aliasing phenomenon by adopting time-varying filtering compared with methods such as EMD, EEMD, and CEEMDAN, and more detailed imfs can be obtained [40,41] without using secondary decomposition. Deep learning has a high dependence on data. For advanced models, large volumes of data are needed for training because more data information further improves the model performance. But for carbon price forecasting, obtaining more real data sets is difficult. Data augmentation (DA) techniques, usually used to solve problems like the inability to expand more real data sets [42], have been empirically illustrated in many studies. DA in time series can improve the stability, generalization, and accuracy of the model compared with using only real data[43–45]. Given that the carbon market is an emerging market, carbon price data are not perfect, the data volume is only 2000 to 3000; with its dramatic volatility and the disruption of outliers, the deep learning model falling into overfitting easily occurs [46], reducing the model performance. The use of DA technology in carbon price forecasting is therefore appropriate. DA can expand the training set to weaken the disruption of outliers to avoid overfitting. Finally, studies have focused on improving the forecasting precision and have only emphasized the evaluation results when comparing the performance while ignoring the real performance of the model in trading in carbon market, raising questions about the practical application of the model.

From the problems and challenges faced in carbon price forecasting, we propose a novel hybrid model based on the decompositionreconstruction idea to further improve the carbon prediction accuracy. First, given the excellent performance of deep learning techniques in carbon price forecasting, with the continuous development of deep learning techniques, Transformer based on the attention mechanism and seq2seq structure [47]has shown excellent performance in time series tasks [48,49]. Therefore, an advanced deep learning technique called Informer is selected as a prediction model. Informer effectively improves Transformer, and compared with LSTM, Informer can better extract the long time dependence in the sequence. In addition, because Informer has more hyperparameters to set, an efficient hyperparameter optimization algorithm, called Bayesian optimization and Hyperband (BOHB), is used to optimize the hyperparameters in Informer. Second, in view of the idea of reconstruction-decomposition, TVFEMD is used as the decomposition method of carbon price to obtain more detailed imfs, and time series clustering is used to classify and reconstruct imfs to avoid the subjectivity of imf division. The high-frequency components of the imfs are retained one by one, while the other components are summed and merged, which not only retains the high-frequency characteristics of the carbon price but also reduces the accumulation of errors and computational time consumption. Subsequently, data augment techniques based on generative algorithms are used to expand the training dataset of each component. The expanded dataset is put into Informer for training and obtaining the prediction results, and each prediction result is integrated to obtain the final prediction results. Finally, a simple and practical trading strategy has been constructed through the proposed model, which has been validated for its potential practical application in the carbon market. On this basis, the main innovations and contributions of this study are as follows.

(1) Considering the subjectivity of the current imfs reconstruction and the repetitiveness of the secondary decomposition, this study improves this based on time series clustering and TVFEMD, which is more objective than the entropy-based reconstruction and avoids the secondary decomposition because more detailed imfs can be obtained by TVFEMD.   
(2) A generative data augment technique was used to extend the carbon price training set to examine the effectiveness of DA in carbon price prediction. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to use DA techniques in carbon price forecasting.   
(3) Informer, an advanced time series forecasting technology, is used, which shows better performance in the time series task by extracting the temporal dependencies in the decomposed reconstructed subcomponents, and optimizing the hyperparameters in Informer by BOHB.   
(4) The proposed hybrid model shows higher prediction accuracy compared with the benchmark model in multiple carbon price datasets as well as different metrics, in addition to further validating the stability of the proposed model through extended experiments.   
(5) The trading strategy constructed based on the proposed hybrid model has shown excellent profitability in various carbon markets, potentially generating more income for investors compared with the baseline trading strategy, as well as avoiding risks and preventing more asset losses.

# 2. Methodology

Fig. 1 shows the main framework of the hybrid carbon price forecasting model Informer-DA-BOHB-TVFEMD-CL designed in this study. The collected carbon price data are first decomposed using TVFEMD to obtain multiple imfs. These imfs are then clustered using time series clustering, and those clustered to the high-frequency component of the imfs are retained, while those clustered to the low-frequency component of the imfs are merged and reconstructed according to their different classes. Then, the reconstructed components are divided into training, validation, and test sets, and the training set is expanded using generative data augmentation techniques. Subsequently, the training and validation sets are used to optimize the hyperparameters of Informer by BOHB. Finally, comparative analysis of prediction results is conducted on a test set. To fully compare the performance of the hybrid models proposed in this study, several forecasting models, namely, extreme learning machine (EL), temporal convolutional neural network (TCN), and long-short term neural network (LSTM), which have performed well in carbon price forecasting, are chosen as benchmark models. EMD and CEEMDAN are used as comparative decomposition algorithms, in addition, a comparison of trading strategies and a variety of extended experiments were conducted. A description of each method used in this study follows.

# 2.2. Bayesian optimization and hyperband

The performance of deep learning has a large dependence on its hyperparameters [51]. Informer’s structure contains a large number of hyperparameters such as the number of encoding and decoding layers, the number of attention mechanism heads, and the number of neurons. Different hyperparameter configurations may lead to different training results. Therefore, the hyperparameter configurations of the model need to be selected reasonably, and a trade-off between performance and efficiency needs to be taken when performing hyperparameter optimization. For example, the grid search method, which selects the optimal hyperparameter configurations by iterating through all possible hyperparameter configurations, is appropriate for small-scale training, but it will result in a huge loss of efficiency when applied to a complex model structure. Some studies have used heuristic algorithms such as genetic algorithms [24] and particle swarm algorithms [52] to optimize hyperparameters. Heuristic algorithms improve the efficiency of hyperparameter optimization, but the convergence of each operation varies, potentially leading to large differences in the results. In this study, an automated hyperparameter tuning model Bayesian optimization and Hyperband (BOHB) [53] is adopted considering the problems in hyperparameter optimization. BOHB combines the advantages of Bayesian optimization (BO) [54] and Hyperband (HB) [55], where HB determines the budget resources used for each BO optimization to reduce the computation time and BO determines the parameters of the next iteration after each HB iteration to avoid the uncertainty caused by random sampling. BO in BOHB uses a similar approach to model the density function of the input configuration as the tree parzen estimator (TPE) [56], the mathematical formula is as follows:

# 2.3. Time-varying filtering for empirical mode decomposition

TVFEMD was proposed by Ref. [40] to improve the empirical modal decomposition. Mode aliasing occurs in the imfs obtained from decomposition in EMD and EEMD, which does not guarantee that only one amplitude mode exists in an imf. The cubic spline interpolation of the upper and lower envelopes used in EMD is not mathematically rigorous in determining the local mean, leading to the obtained imfs potentially not having a clear physical meaning. TVFEMD improves the performance of frequency separation and stability at low sampling rates, solves the problem of mode aliasing, and retains the time-varying characteristics of the time series, and the meaning of the TVFEMD parameter is clear and simple to choose [57]. This results in the imfs obtained by TVFEMD being able to better represent the mode characteristics in the time series. The specific computational steps of TVFEMD are as follows:

The instantaneous amplitude $\mathsf { A } ( t )$ and frequency b of the original sequence $x ( t )$ are obtained by Hibbert transform, and subsequently, the local maximum $\mathsf { A } ( t _ { m a x } )$ and local minimum $\mathrm { A } ( t _ { m i n } )$ of $\mathsf { A } ( t )$ are obtained. $\eta _ { I } ( t )$ and $\eta _ { 2 } ( t )$ are obtained by interpolation of $\mathrm { A } _ { 2 } ( t _ { m a x } )$ and $\mathsf { A } _ { 2 } ( t _ { m i n } )$ .

$$

# 2.4. Time series clustering

Clustering methods are based on the similarity between datasets, grouping the most similar data into a class. Distance is commonly used as a measure of similarity, e.g., Euclidean distance (ED) [58]. For time series, ED cannot easily measure the nonlinear relationship between two time series as well as time skewness, especially for complex high-frequency time series, e.g., two time series with similar fluctuation characteristics. If at each time step t their fluctuations are in opposite directions, their evaluation by ED may classify these two time series into two classes because ED directly calculates the distance between the corresponding time points of the two time series, and fails to deal with the time step inconsistency, and thus is not applicable to imfs series with different fluctuation characteristics. Therefore, this study uses dynamic time-warping (DTW) [59] to measure the similarity between two imfs. DTW minimizes the Euclidean distance by point mapping between two time series. Unlike with ED, which is a one-to-one match in calculating the distance, DTW matches the different time points by dynamic matching to minimize the Euclidean distance. The formula for DTW is as follows:

$$
d _ { D T W } \big ( T ^ { 1 } , T ^ { 2 } \big ) = \bigg ( \sum \frac { m _ { \tau } l c m ( k ) ^ { e } } { M _ { \tau } } \bigg ) ^ { \frac { 1 } { e } } , \forall k \in \tau
$$

This equation is the optimal path obtained by matching the two time series, e corresponds to the $l _ { e }$ norm, $l c m ( k )$ is the local cost matrix, $k$ represents the minimized alignment path of the two time series $T ^ { l }$ and $T ^ { 2 }$ , and the DTW finds the optimal path by dynamic programming. A simple k-means [60] clustering method applying the DTW distance was used to cluster the imfs. An improved clustering algorithm based on $\mathbf { k }$ -means was chosen to measure the similarity between sequences and thus classify imfs by DTW. The DTW between each sequence is first computed and the initial center is randomly selected among the sequences, the sequences are assigned to the class of the nearest center, and the centers of the clusters are recomputed by DTWBA [58]. Based on the distance between each sequence and each center, the sequence is reassigned to the class to which the nearest center belongs by iterating until the center converges or the maximum number of iteration steps is reached.

# 2.5. Time series data augment

Commonly used time series augment includes adding white noise to the series [61] or slicing [62] or scaling [63] the time series. However, these simple data augmentation methods are flawed in that they assume noise in the data, time invariance, or a greater focus on the shape characteristics. These assumptions are not consistent with the characteristics of financial time series. Therefore, this study uses variational autoencoders (VAEs) as a time series augment technique. The structure of VAEs is shown in Fig. 1, divided into encoder, decoder, and latent sampling layers, which is a special type of autoencoder that learns the representation of the true observation $x$ in the latent space $\mathfrak { z }$ by modeling the probability distributions of the observation and latent variables [64]. To learn these representations, VAEs use variational inference to approximate the true unknown posterior distribution $p ( z / x )$ , by a controlled distribution $q _ { \lambda } ( z / x )$ , with λ being the set of variational parameters. VAEs are trained by minimizing the reconstruction loss (to make the decoded output as close as possible to the original input) and the latent loss (to make the encoded latent variables as close as possible to the pre-determined prior distribution), as in Equation (19). This allows VAEs to efficiently learn the underlying structure of the data and generate new datasets similar to the training data.

$$
\arg \operatorname* { m i n } _ { \phi , \theta } \mathcal { L } ( \phi , \theta ; x ) = - \left[ \mathbb { E } _ { z \sim \mathbf { q } _ { \phi ( z | x ) } } \lbrack \log { \mathfrak { p } _ { \theta } ( \mathbf { x } | \mathbf { z } ) } \right] - \mathrm { D _ { K L } } \bigg [ \mathbf { q } _ { \phi } ( \mathbf { z } | \mathbf { x } ) \| \ p ( \mathbf { z } ) \bigg ] \bigg ]
$$

where the first term is the minimized reconstruction loss and the second term is the potential loss which controls the structure of $\mathbf { z }$ and distinguishes VAEs from the autoencoder [65]. The VAEs are trained by minimizing the loss in Eq. (19). After the training is completed, they are sampled in the latent space z using the prior distribution $\mathfrak { p } ( \mathbf { z } )$ , followed by data augmentation by a generative network, also known as a decoder.

To effectively evaluate the proposed hybrid model, four carbon markets are selected as experimental subjects: EU-ETS, Guangdong carbon pilot market, Hubei carbon pilot market, and Chinese national carbon market. Large fluctuations occur in each carbon market, and the span of carbon price is also large, and the distribution is more complex. According to the need for model training, the dataset is divided into a training set, a validation set, and a test set with a 7:1:2 ratio.

# 3.2. Evaluation measures

To objectively react to the prediction errors of different models, the evaluation indicators of absolute value error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and goodnessof-fit $( \mathsf { R } ^ { 2 } )$ were chosen. The formulas were calculated as follows:

$$
M A E { = } { \frac { 1 } { n } } { \sum } _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } { | y _ { i } - { \widehat { y } } _ { i } | }
$$

$$
R M S E = \sqrt { \frac { 1 } { n } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ( y _ { i } - \widehat { y } _ { i } ) } ^ { 2 } }
$$

$$
R ^ { 2 } = 1 - \frac { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ( y _ { i } - \widehat { y } _ { i } ) ^ { 2 } } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ( y _ { i } - \overline { { y } } _ { i } ) ^ { 2 } }
$$

Here, a is the true value, b is the predicted value and n is the number of predicted values.

The stochastic nature of deep learning when training and optimizing leads to uncertainty in the results. These metrics cannot reflect this uncertainty in prediction, especially when the evaluation criteria of the two models are very similar. The two models cannot be judged by the evaluation metrics values alone, because the difference in prediction error between the two models may not be significant [46]. Therefore, the Diebold–Mariano test (DM test) [66] statistic was also used to test whether a significant difference occurs in the predictive ability between the models. The null hypothesis $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 0 } \right)$ of the DM test is that the two models have the same predictive performance, while the alternative hypothesis $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 1 } \right)$ is the opposite. The DM test value is calculated in the following equation.

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \displaystyle { D M = \sqrt { n } \ \frac { \overline { { d } } } { \sigma } \sim N ( 0 , 1 ) } } \\ { \displaystyle { \overline { { d } } = \frac { 1 } { n } \sum _ { i } ^ { n } d _ { i } , d _ { i } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \Big | y _ { i } - \widehat { y } _ { i j } \Big | - \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \big | y _ { i } - \widehat { y } _ { i , k } \big | , j \neq k } } \end{array}
$$

where $j$ and $k$ represent the predicted results of the different models, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of $d _ { i }$ .

# 4.2. Experimental results and analysis

In this study, the training set was expanded by a factor of five through DA. The lag step was set to 30 to cover more temporal information due to Informer’s powerful ability to handle long time series. Informer’s hyperparameter optimizations include the number of encoder layers and decoder layers $( \mathrm { l } _ { \mathrm { n } } \in [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] )$ , batch size $( \mathbf { b _ { n } ~ \in ~ }$ [16,32,64,128]), number of hidden layer features $( \mathbf { f } _ { \mathrm { n } } \in [ 1 6 , 3 2 , 6 4 , 1 2 8 ]$ ), head numbers $( \mathsf { h } _ { \mathtt { n } } \in [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] )$ , and learning rate $( \mathrm { l r } \in \ [ 0 { - } 1 ] )$ , these hyperparameters have a significant impact on Informer [50]. The budget for BOHB, i.e., the epochs of Informer, was set to 500, taking into account precision and time. All experiments were performed on a personal laptop with Python 3.8.3 and Matlab R2022a, with an AMD R9 7945HX, 96 GB of RAM, and a single Nvidia GeForce RTX 4080 GPU. Four comparisons were made based on the experimental results. Comparison I compares the model performance before and after DA. Comparison II analyzes the prediction performance of Informer. Comparison III compares different decomposition algorithms. Comparison IV validates the feasibility of improved decomposition–reconstruction.

Comparison I: In this study, DA was used to expand the training set. Deep learning needs to fit the nonlinear features from a large amount of data, and some of the disturbances such as outliers or frequent cluster fluctuations can lead to model overfitting. # 4.3. Trading strategy

These experiments validate the superiority of the proposed hybrid model in terms of prediction accuracy, but pursuing only a high prediction accuracy while ignoring the performance in real trading will limit the model’s use. Therefore, this study constructs a simple but effective buy–sell strategy [69,70]based on the proposed hybrid model to verify that an accurate carbon price forecasting model can bring higher returns and lower investment risks for investors. We assume that the investor owns a carbon asset of 1 at the initial moment t. The investor compares the predicted carbon price $\widehat { y } _ { t + 1 }$ with the current carbon closing price $y _ { t }$ . If $\widehat { y } _ { t + 1 } \geq y _ { t }$ , then the investor continues to hold the carbon allowances. If $\widehat { y } _ { t + 1 } < y _ { t } ,$ , then the investor sells the carbon allowances at the closing price at the $t$ period and holds the cash. While holding cash, if $\widehat { y } _ { t + 1 } \geq y _ { t }$ , then the investor purchases carbon allowances at the closing price at time t. If $\widehat { y } _ { t + 1 } < y _ { t }$ , then the investor continues to hold cash. In addition, to be more in line with the actual transaction, transaction costs are set. The classic buy and hold (BH) strategy was chosen as the benchmark strategy, where carbon assets are held in BH at the beginning and remain throughout the trading period. We simulated trading in EU-ETS, Guangdong, and Hubei carbon markets, because in the Chinese national carbon market, we found that the revenue of all models was lower than the BH strategy. This was because the poor fitting results of the Chinese carbon market could not support the construction of the trading strategy. A deeper reason was the insufficient amount of data.

According to the constructed trading strategy, the simulation is carried out in the test set, and the cumulative returns of each carbon market are shown in Fig. 11. The performance of the constructed hybrid model in the three carbon markets shows strong profitability, and almost all of them are optimal. This also verifies that the constructed accurate carbon price prediction model can bring investors excessive returns, and the cumulative return in EU-ETS reaches $6 0 0 ~ \%$ , which is much more than the BH strategy. In Guangdong carbon market, all the models react to the initial price drop to avoid the corresponding risk for investors, showing that the constructed models not only achieve considerable accuracy in carbon price prediction but also show good results in practical application.

# 4.4.2. Validation of retaining and reconstructing

First, the treatment of retaining the high-frequency component and reconstructing the low-frequency component of the imfs obtained from the TVFEMD decomposition was further verified. For the retained part, the prediction results of each imfs are summarized, and the prediction results for the high-frequency and low-frequency components are shown in Table 6. A comparison of the results in Table 6 shows that the prediction results between the retained and reconstructed high-frequency components are obvious, and the error of the retained high-frequency component is significantly smaller than the reconstructed highfrequency component in the four experiments. MAE decreased from 1.7050 to 0.2008 in EU-ETS, from 1.0381 to 0.3050 in the Guangdong carbon market, from 0.5667 to 0.1412 in the Hubei carbon market, and from 0.7238 to 0.1340 in the Chinese national carbon market. This verifies keeping the imfs of the high-frequency components one by one is correct. This also shows that the complex fluctuation characteristics of the high-frequency component need to be represented in a more detailed way and that reconstructing the high-frequency component will make the model lose its accuracy. This also confirms that in some studies [31, 46] merging the high-frequency component may be inappropriate. By contrast, the accuracy of the low-frequency component is improved after reconstruction except for EU-ETS, while the accuracy of EU-ETS is almost the same, showing that the reconstruction of the low-frequency component can reduce the accumulation of errors, whereas the number of times the model is trained is reduced by reconstruction, thereby shrinking the model’s runtime. A comparison of the MAPE of the high-frequency and low-frequency components shows that the former contributes most of the prediction errors. The high-frequency component contributes to the main short-term fluctuations in carbon price series, and these high-frequency signals contain many uncertainties that make the model more difficult to train. By contrast, the low-frequency component responds more to the long-term trend, and the periodic changes have a stronger stability and predictability. Therefore, the high-frequency component is even more important for carbon price forecasting.

# 4.5. Discussion

In the study, Informer was used as a prediction model, and the results illustrated its better performance compared with other deep learning structures such as LSTM and TCN. The finding illustrated that the carbon price sequence needs to rely on longer time steps as feature inputs, which is in line with the conclusions drawn by Ref. [72]. The Transformer-based Informer has a strong capability of long time feature extraction, a model that can cover a wider range of time steps in the prediction of carbon price is needed. Data augmentation plays a certain role in carbon price forecasting because of violent fluctuations in carbon price. The four carbon markets selected have sudden rises and falls, and an aggregation of fluctuations occurs. Deep learning with a large number of parameters will overfit these anomalies, so that the model will be over-learned by the bias patterns [73], leading to a decrease in the generalization ability of the model. Data augmentation can alleviate this phenomenon to a certain extent, and the experimental results also prove this. However, for the Chinese national carbon market, which has a particularly small dataset, the prediction results are not satisfactory even with data augmentation and a series of data processing methods. DA makes the model learn the features in the sequences better, rather than creating new features. A certain amount of data are therefore essential for higher prediction accuracy of carbon price.

![](images/201165255ca86503ed5964dfd5120a68b3e97f46773d2b65edb37bb9c481211e.jpg)  
Fig. 15. Five-step forward prediction DMtest results of four carbon markets.

The idea of dealing with decomposition-reconstruction was based on the previous work, which came from the trade-off between accuracy and the number of decompositions. The experiments indicated that the more detailed the decomposition of imfs, the better the prediction effect, but an excessive number of decompositions would result. Classifying the imfs that had similar fluctuating features was also obvious, which would alleviate the unnecessary cost of time and the accumulation of errors. Entropy-based division can lead to subjective judgments, where imfs are combined into unsuitable feature sequences. By contrast, time series clustering-based approaches can be more objective in dividing imfs. However, for the choice of clustering approach, our results tend to favor approaches that can better reflect the fluctuating characteristics of imfs, such as DTW, while incorrect division may lead to the superposition of different volatility features increasing the complexity leading to the loss of model accuracy. We also demonstrate the importance of highfrequency features for carbon price prediction, which is reflected in the fact that short-term high-frequency fluctuations are more difficult to predict compared with cyclical and trendline fluctuations, contributing to more prediction errors, it’s also because of this more detailed highfrequency features are needed, while low-frequency features can be merged. The importance of the quality of the decomposition is captured in the fact that a good decomposition algorithm TVFEMD, needs only one decomposition to achieve or even surpass the results of a secondary decomposition, e.g., CEEMDAN $^ +$ VMD.

From the economic perspective, under the pressure of global warming, carbon allowances will become a limited resource, and this resource is crucial for energy-intensive industries, such as power, chemical, transportation, and construction. The drastic fluctuation of carbon price will have a significant impact on the emission reduction decisions of these enterprises. Therefore, the practical value of this study is to provide a new hybrid forecasting framework for carbon price forecasting, potentially providing enterprises in the carbon market with appropriate trading strategies to avoid the risk of carbon price fluctuation, and to obtain higher carbon trading returns, especially for those low-carbon enterprises, thereby increasing the value of their carbon assets and motivating them to actively participate in the carbon market. Market managers can also formulate a reasonable carbon quota allocation and pricing mechanism through accurate carbon price forecasts, adjust carbon quotas and carbon policies, balance market supply and demand, and avoid drastic price fluctuations. This will help the lowcarbon transition of high-carbon industries, reduce carbon emissions, and promote environmental sustainability to solve the global climate problem.

# 5. Conclusion

Carbon price forecasting plays an important role in the management of the carbon market, providing the basis for investment, risk management, and policymaking, and ultimately contributing to the achievement of emission reduction targets. In this study, we improved the decomposition–reconstruction by adopting TVFEMD decomposition and time series clustering, used the advanced deep learning model Informer and the hyperparameter optimization technique BOHB, and combined the generative data enhancement technique VAEs to construct a hybrid carbon price prediction model Informer-DA-BOHB-TVFEMD-CL. The proposed hybrid model exhibits higher prediction accuracy compared with the baseline model on the selected experimental subjects and has demonstrated good profitability in carbon trading. Based on the experimental results, the main conclusions of this study are as follows.

(1) Informer is a more stable and competitive prediction model. Compared with EL, TCN, and LSTM, Informer has higher prediction accuracy and shows reliability in different experiments and prediction steps. Hyperparameter optimization is necessary for Informer, and BOHB is able to find a more suitable and efficient model structure than the manually set model structure.   
(2) Expanding the training set through DA can further improve the generalization ability as well as the performance of the model. The significance of DA as an important sample expansion technique in deep learning is demonstrated in carbon price forecasting, but it also requires a certain amount of data to support it.   
(3) Decomposition–reconstruction based on TVFEMD and time-series clustering effectively improves the decomposition–reconstruction approach of the present study. TVFEMD extracts more detailed time-frequency features from the carbon price than EMD, CEEMDAN, and the second decomposition that combines VMD and CEEMDAN. The improvement of the entropybased discrimination method to a time-series clustering method based on DTW distances reduces the subjectivity of imf classification and further improves the prediction accuracy.   
(4) The time–frequency features of the high-frequency component are more important for carbon price prediction, which is also the reason why many studies use second decomposition. For the high-frequency features, reconstruction will lead to a significant increase in the prediction error due to the increase in the complexity of the features. Therefore, the high-frequency component is not suitable for reconstruction, and these imfs need to be retained. The time-frequency features of the lowfrequency component are obvious, and even if they are reconstructed, their complexity will not increase, so they can be reconstructed. The accumulation of errors, as well as time costs, can be effectively reduced.

(5) The proposed hybrid model shows good profitability in carbon trading. The use of the proposed hybrid model for carbon quota trading can bring more profit for enterprises, motivating them to participate in the carbon market. Enterprises also will actively reduce carbon emissions to obtain more profit, thereby realizing sustainable transformation.

The model proposed in this study has high accuracy and stability in carbon price prediction. However, room for improvement and avenues for further research remain. First, this study uses VAEs as a data augment technique. VAEs have a priori distributional assumptions about the training samples, leading to potential biases in the extended dataset. Many advanced data enhancement techniques that do not require prior knowledge are available, such as generative adversarial networks (GAN) and comparative learning, so we encourage more researchers to validate them. Second, the high-frequency component of imfs is important for carbon price prediction, which can be more finely divided using some improved decomposition algorithms. Third, accurate carbon price models should serve carbon market participants, not only in trading strategies, and their use deserves further expansion.
