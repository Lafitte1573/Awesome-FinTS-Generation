Over the past several years, a growing number of researchers and investors have displayed a keen interest in predicting stock price movements, actively conducting research that leverages cutting-edge deep learning models to capture the complexities of the stock market. In contrast, research on predicting price movements in the futures market, which is essential for risk management and forecasting the future value of underlying assets, is nearly absent. In this study, we propose a robust and efficient algorithmic trading model designed for the futures market, which exhibits significant time-series volatility, similar to the stock market. We assess the model’s performance in both the Korean and U.S. markets. Using time-series generative adversarial networks (TimeGAN), we augment time-series data into three dimensions and employ a 3-dimensional convolutional neural network (3DCNN) to identify temporal correlations within a multidimensional spatial structure. We thoroughly assess the performance of the proposed model by subjecting it to rigorous backtesting within a simulated environment that closely emulates real-world conditions in the futures market. The results demonstrate improvements of approximately 1.35 times in risk-adjusted returns and up to 6390 times in cost efficiency over both short- and long-term timeframes. This demonstrates that the TimeGAN augmentation and 3D expansion method outperforms existing methodologies in terms of risk-adjusted returns and cost efficiency, effectively capturing the volatility of the futures market. The main objective of this research is to provide investors with a more proficient decision-making tool through algorithmic trading that seamlessly functions in the real-world futures market.

# 1. Introduction

Financial markets exert significant influence over a wide range of domains, including business, education, employment, technology, and the overall economy [1]. Over the years, investors and researchers have sought to develop and evaluate models that elucidate the behavior of stock prices [2]. Nevertheless, analyzing stock market movements and price behaviors is challenging, given the market’s volatility, nonlinearity, nonstationarity, and nonparametric nature and the presence of noise [3]. As a result, as artificial intelligence has progressed, deep learning models for time series have been proposed, demonstrating notable efficiency gains compared to traditional statistical models [4–8].

While research in the stock market is thriving, studies predicting price movements in the futures market are notably lacking. Futures represent a type of derivative that allows for high-leverage trading based on predictions of underlying asset price fluctuations [9]. Typically, the futures market is more liquid than the stock market and plays an essential role in risk management rather than speculative actions. Thus, predicting movements in the futures market can be even more critical than predicting stock market movement. Cuny [10]. In particular, index futures offer insight into the future value of the stock market and can serve as a means to mitigate risks associated with stock market investment. Therefore, predicting the future value of index futures is paramount. From an investor’s perspective, making precise predictions about the price movements of derivatives such as futures enables attaining greater excess returns through leveraged investments compared to other financial products. However, most ongoing research on the futures market is limited to the commodity futures market [11–14]. Thus, there is an evident need for a systematic and objective approach to predict movements in index futures.

As in the stock market, predicting movements in the futures market is challenging due to significant time-series noise and restricted data availability. Unlike the stock market, the futures market has expiration dates, necessitating rollovers to subsequent contracts at each expiration. Consequently, as the rollover period approaches, trading volumes fluctuate sharply. Therefore, developing predictive models that capture and reflect these time-series complexities is essential. While deep learning in other domains, such as images and text, has utilized data augmentation to improve performance, time series prediction, which involves capturing temporal dependencies, presents challenges in regard to applying data augmentation [15].

Considering this background, this research aims to propose an algorithm for predicting the movement of futures prices using the TimeGAN time-series data augmentation technique and to offer investors a more proficient decision-making tool through algorithmic trading that seamlessly functions in the real-world futures market. The contributions of this paper are tow below:

1. The augmented data are expanded into three dimensions, and a 3DCNN model is applied to identify the temporal correlations in a multidimensional spatial structure. This approach enables the effective identification of uncertainties at each point in time, which could not be captured due to the independence issue of samples when augmented in the form of existing samples. In other words, by reflecting various scenarios that can occur at each point in time, it is possible to minimize the uncertainty that arises when predicting with only one figure per time point.

2. We propose a new trading strategy aimed at achieving high returns on investment with low volatility by predicting movements in the futures market. The practicality of the proposed model is evaluated through backtesting on KOSPI 200, S&P 500, and NASDAQ 100 futures markets, comparing it with existing time-series augmentation methods such as RNN-GAN and LSTM-GAN. This evaluation considers all typical conditions found in real futures investments, including margin requirements and contract sizes.

# 2. Literature review

# 2.1. Forecasting stock price

Research aimed at predicting stock prices has long been a subject of interest among researchers. Early studies primarily employed technologies such as rough sets, probabilistic neural networks, and fuzzy techniques [16–23].

Recently, research has progressed in predicting the direction or future prices of the stock market using various machine learning techniques and ensemble methods. Yakup et al. (2011) proposed two models based on artificial neural networks (ANNs) and support vector machines (SVMs) for predicting the movement direction of the Istanbul Stock Exchange (ISE) National 100 Index. They selected ten technical indicators as inputs and measured performance. The experimental results indicated that the ANN model significantly outperformed the SVM model. Basak et al. [24] conducted tests on various companies using the random forest and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models to predict whether stock prices would rise or fall compared to their prices over the preceding n days, reporting enhanced accuracy compared to the existing prediction performance.

Research actively employing machine learning methodologies in conjunction with traditional time series models has also been actively progressing. Zhang [25] proposed a hybrid methodology that leverages the unique strengths of both the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model and the artificial neural network (ANN) model to overcome the limitation of ARIMA in easily capturing nonlinear patterns. Pai & Lin [26] introduced a hybrid model that combines the ARIMA model with the support vector machine (SVM) model.

The advancement of deep learning has led to active research in applying deep learning models to predict stock prices, particularly in the context of highly nonlinear financial data, demonstrating superior performance compared to traditional research. Fischer & Krauss [27] conducted a study using long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to predict directional movements for the constituent stocks of the S&P 500. Long et al. [28] introduced a novel end-to-end model called the multifilter neural network (MFNN), designed for feature extraction and price movement prediction in financial time series samples. Their research surpassed existing models in terms of accuracy, profitability, and stability. Research focused on index futures is as follows: Zhang et al. [29] predicted index futures using a hybrid approach that combined ARIMA-LSTM and Wavelet Transform, demonstrating robust predictive performance even amid the shocks caused by COVID-19. Liang & Jia [30] proposed a model that combines online response data with financial market data to predict the futures market, showing significant improvements in performance.

To date, research focused on implementing new deep learning models in the stock market continues to progress steadily [31–35].

# 2.2. Data augmentation

Deep learning models require that a significant number of parameters be learned, rendering them dependent on extensive training data. However, many real-world tasks suffer from a shortage of training data. Data augmentation refers to techniques used to address the problem of limited training data by generating new data or expanding the dataset through data transformations. The progress of deep learning models has spurred extensive research in the field of data augmentation.

Data augmentation techniques have primarily been studied in the computer vision domain. [36] compared the performance of various image data augmentation techniques applied to three medical case studies: skin melanoma diagnosis, histopathological image analysis, and breast magnetic resonance imaging scan analysis. [37] conducted benchmarking of different data augmentation methods to determine the most suitable approaches for enhancing the performance of convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

Research on the application of various data augmentation techniques for time series data has also been actively pursued. Lee & Kim [38] introduced a data augmentation and normalization framework called columnwise random shuffling; it is applicable to Nunet and CNN, which are innovative frameworks for successful learning of high-level features from ultrahigh-dimensional time series data for stock market prediction. Semenoglou et al. [39] proposed that incorporating data augmentation techniques, ranging from basic transformations and adjustments to advanced generative models, in combination with a novel upsampling approach, can significantly enhance predictive performance when used alongside deep neural networks.

Recently, there has been research on data augmentation using generative adversarial network (GAN) models, and GANs have also been applied to augment time series data. [40] proposed the MM-GAN model for MRI augmentation, aimed at filling training sets by sampling new data from constrained data distributions. Mogren [41] proposed the RNN-GAN model, which combines RNN and GAN, and demonstrated its superior performance in generating classical music. Yoon et al. [42] were the first to introduce the time-series generative adversarial network (TimeGAN) framework, which preserves temporal dynamics in generated time-series data. TimeGAN combines unsupervised learning flexibility with the control typically offered by supervised training. In efforts to enhance predictive accuracy, Zhang et al. [43] augmented data with TimeGAN and applied it to SVR, XGB, and RF, comparing the results to using only the original data.

# 3. Methodology

# 3.1. Time-series generative adversarial networks

Time-series data are characterized by their inherent temporal dependencies and distinctive correlations over time, creating a challenge for conventional generative adversarial networks (GANs) to model effectively. GANs do not inherently possess a built-in understanding of the unique temporal correlations of time-series data. GANs independently process each data point without taking into account the essential sequential dependencies that are often critical for modeling time-series data. To overcome this limitation and proficiently handle time-series data, researchers have developed specialized variations of GANs and alternative deep learning architectures. TimeGAN [42] introduces the use of a stepwise supervised loss in addition to the unsupervised adversarial loss, allowing it to capture stepwise conditional distributions of the data. It employs embedding networks to reduce the high dimensionality of the adversarial learning space. This is the key difference from existing time-series GAN models. The supervised loss enables robust generation by reflecting the time-series data at the next time step, and by generating data in a low-dimensional embedding space, it prevents overfitting caused by noise in the time-series data.

TimeGAN consists of four parts, as shown in Fig. 1. The embedding layer maps the data $\left( X _ { 1 : t } \right)$ into a latent space, assisting the model in learning the temporal dynamics of the data. The recovery layer restores the embedded data $( h _ { 1 : t } )$ back into the feature space $( \widetilde X _ { 1 : t } )$ . Instead of directly generating into the feature space, the generator takes a random vector $( z _ { 1 : t } )$ as input and generates into the embedding space $( \widehat { h } _ { 1 : t } )$ . The discriminator operates in the embedding space and classifies the input data as real or generated $( \widetilde { y } _ { 1 : t } )$ .

To effectively capture the characteristics of time series data, TimeGAN combines four components and introduces three loss functions. The reconstruction loss $( { \mathcal { L } } _ { R } )$ is utilized to guarantee the accurate encoding and decoding of the original data by the embedding layer and recovery layer. The L2-norm of the difference between the real data $\left( x _ { t } \right)$ and the data after passing through the embedding layer and recovery layer $( \widetilde { x } _ { 1 : t } )$ is used as the loss. The unsupervised loss $( \mathcal { L } _ { U } )$ is similar to the loss used in traditional GANs. The vectors of $y _ { t }$ and $\widehat { \boldsymbol { y } } _ { t }$ are the discriminator’s predictions for the real data and the generated data, respectively. If the discriminator predicts that the data are real, it will be close to 1. The supervised loss $( \mathcal { L } _ { s } )$ is designed to aid the generator in learning temporal relationships from the data by comparing the generated data at each time step to the actual data. The generator $( g _ { x } ( h _ { t - 1 } , z _ { t } ) )$ takes the embedded data $( h _ { t - 1 } )$ from the previous time point and a new random vector $\left( \boldsymbol { z } _ { t } \right)$ to generate a value, which is then compared with the true embedded data $\left( h _ { t } \right)$ . # 3.2. 3D-CNNpred model

Hoseinzade & Haratizadeh [44] proposed a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based framework for stock market prediction, as shown in Fig. 2. This framework encompasses the extraction of features from diverse sources, including various markets. Among the models they introduced, the 3D-CNNpred model utilizes a three-dimensional tensor to incorporate information from multiple markets simultaneously. The dataset comprises data from i distinct markets, $\mathbf { k }$ variables, and information from the past j days.

The 3D-CNNpred model proceeds with final prediction after undergoing daily feature extraction and durational feature extraction. In daily feature extraction, it computes $^ \textrm { \scriptsize 1 } \times 1$ convolutional filters $\left( w _ { 1 \mathrm { x 1 } } \right)$ with a depth of k on the 3D input tensor $( \nu _ { i , j , k } )$ , serving as a feature selection and extraction algorithm for the data. In durational feature extraction, filters of $_ { 3 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } }$ (the number of markets) are applied to summarize data fluctuation patterns over time. Subsequently, utilizing several max pooling and convolutional layers, the extracted feature maps are employed for the ultimate prediction.

# 3.3. TG-3DCNN model

This paper introduces the TG-3DCNN model, which combines TimeGAN and 3D-CNNpred. TG-3DCNN is designed to handle time series data characterized by substantial volatility. The proposed model leverages past data to train TimeGAN to recognize the temporal patterns inherent in time series data. Using the trained TimeGAN model, synthetic data and real data are stacked in a 3D format and input into the prediction model.

The data used for TimeGAN training consist of a 2D dataset with a shape of a $\left( \operatorname { t } \mathbf { x } \mathbf { k } \right)$ matrix, encompassing information for k variables over t time steps. For the generation process, a random variable z is drawn from a uniform distribution in the range [0,1] with the shape of a $\left( \mathbf { t } \mathbf { x } \mathbf { k } \right)$ matrix. The random variable z is passed through an encoder, resulting in an embedding matrix with a shape of (t x e). This mapping into the latent space facilitates the adversarial network’s ability to learn the temporal dynamics of the data through low-dimensional representations. By inputting the data into an encoder configured to a specific dimension, we can generate augmented data from the latent vector rather than from the original time-series data, which contains a lot of noise.

The overall process of the proposed model for predicting futures price movement direction is illustrated in Fig. 3. First, based on the TimeGAN model trained on the Train period data, a large number of synthetic data are generated for the Test period data. These synthetic data are then structured in a 2D form, such as $\left( \operatorname { t } \mathbf { x } \mathbf { k } \right)$ , and A-1 of them are selected. Here, A-1 (augmentation-1) synthetic data are chosen based on their similarity to real data, starting with the data most similar to the original data and stacking them sequentially in the form of a 3D tensor. The 3D tensor has the form of $( \mathbf { t } \ \mathbf { x } \ \mathbf { k } \ \mathbf { x } \ \mathbf { A } )$ , and the mean squared error (MSE) is used as the similarity metric to ensure that augmented data similar to the original data are used. Finally, this 3D tensor is used as input for the 3DCNN to predict whether the price will rise or fall the next day.

The 3D stacking method is beneficial for the following reasons: 1) Augmenting data to increase sample size typically treats augmented data independently, meaning that the interactions between similar sequences cannot be effectively communicated to the model. 2) The 3D stacking approach enables the model to process interactions between similar sequences in a dependent manner by utilizing augmented data generated to resemble the original data. Instead of relying on a single randomly determined value in noisy time-series data, the model analyzes in a dependent manner within a dimension composed of a combination of randomly selected values most similar to the original. This allows the model to preserve the inherent temporal correlations of the data while simultaneously learning to be robust against noise.

The prediction model utilizes a 3DCNN architecture, which is highly suitable for multidimensional time-series analysis as it effectively captures spatial and temporal features along with their correlations. By discerning the correlations between real and synthetic data, the model better captures the temporal characteristics and patterns inherent in time-series data compared to using only real data as input.

# 4.3. Step 3&4: experimental setting

GAN-based models commence its training with a 20-year dataset, spanning from January 8, 1997, to December 31, 2016, and subsequently rebalances and accumulates data on an annual basis. Therefore, GAN-based models learning for evaluation have a total of 5 testing years, from models learned from 2016 to 2020. The performance evaluation assesses the model’s ability to predict the upward or downward movement of the next day’s futures index for each respective period.

Determination of the Optimal Split. To precisely measure the performance of the prediction models utilizing augmented time series data generated by TimeGAN, we conduct cross-validation using the sliding window technique. Prior to testing the prediction models, we determine the optimal training-validation-test period to achieve the best model performance. We consider 24 combinations, where the training period ranges from 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months; the validation period is set to 1 or 2 months; and the test period is 1 or 2 months. To select the optimal period, we randomly sample 2 months from the 5 testing years, comparing the prediction accuracy for a total of 10 months. We determine the data split period based on the performance for these 10 months, selecting the 6:2:1 ratio, which exhibits the best prediction performance among the aggregated data for different ratios. The details of the randomly selected periods are provided in Table 5 below.

Investment Simulation. Following the determination of the data split period, we select the optimal algorithmic trading model for futures investment. In the stock market, actual assets are traded, while in the futures market, contracts about future prices are traded. Margin is a deposit required when a trader trades stocks or futures, which is a certain percentage of the contract amount. Especially in the futures market, due to the potential for large losses from price fluctuations, there is a system called ‘margin call’ that requires additional margin depending on price changes. To do so, we backtest prediction models based on previously described performance metrics, including returns, volatility, and the Sharpe ratio. Notably, our experiments incorporate the mechanism of margin calls, which adjust according to price fluctuations. Backtesting is performed using each model’s signals in a real futures market environment, and the monthly investment performance, 5-year cumulative returns, costs, and other factors are used to select the final model. Detailed information on the period used in the experiment is shown in Table 6 below. Similar to the data split period phase, TimeGAN is initially trained on 20 years of data for the testing year and subsequently rebalances and accumulates data on an annual basis. The predictive models are evaluated in a sliding window by using a 6:2:1 period for one year after the TimeGAN learning period. Therefore, investment performance over 5 years is evaluated for a total of 5 testing years, one year for each testing year. The selected 5 testing years were chosen because the remaining period, after excluding the 20 years required for sufficient training of TimeGAN, amounts to 5 years. This 5- year period is sufficiently long to yield meaningful results; however, it has the limitation of not fully accounting for the diverse market conditions that have occurred historically (e.g., the 2008 financial crisis). Nevertheless, since the 5 testing years include the COVID-19 pandemic period, which encompasses both recessionary and booming phases, this limitation is partially mitigated.

# 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new algorithmic trading model targeting the KOSPI200 futures market. By applying the time series augmentation technique TimeGAN and extending it into 3 dimensions using 3DCNN, we develop a robust, superior, and efficient model that effectively captures multidimensional information from the data. According to the experimental results, the proposed 3-dimensional extended model outperforms the traditional unaugmented stock prediction model. Additionally, the sample augmentation model, which increases the data sample count through time-series augmentation, fails to surpass the baseline model, and existing time-series augmentation methods such as RNN-GAN and LSTM-GAN demonstrate lower performance compared to TimeGAN, as they do not effectively capture the complexity of timeseries data. The proposed 10TG-3DCNN model achieves the highest short-term returns and risk-adjusted returns, while also securing significant long-term gains. This is evidenced by a performance improvement of approximately 1.35 times compared to the second-best model in terms of risk-adjusted returns. Additionally, it demonstrates up to 6390 times greater efficiency in terms of data size and training time, making it more efficient than other models and a practical solution for both performance improvement and cost reduction.

Financial markets feature various traded financial instruments, with futures transactions being particularly appealing to investors in these markets. This paper demonstrates that simple data augmentation can lead to reduced performance and increased costs. In contrast, expanding the augmentation into three dimensions shows greater cost-effectiveness and better performance compared to existing methods. This suggests that using the proposed model to predict futures market movements, based on TimeGAN and 3D-CNN, allows investors in various futures markets to create more efficient portfolios. Additionally, we perform backtesting, reflecting real futures market conditions such as margins and contract sizes, to evaluate its applicability to index futures, which are beneficial for both investing and hedging. This approach aims to provide valuable investment guidance to investors involved in futures trading.

One limitation of this study is that, due to the computational demands of augmentation, we were unable to explore various augmentation sizes extensively. From the perspective of dimensional expansion, experiments with augmentation sizes of 10, 100, and 500 revealed that models using augmentations of 100 and 500 exhibited poorer performance. This suggests that excessive augmentation may introduce substantial bias, as the augmented data is artificially generated. Consequently, future research will focus on expanding experiments with smaller augmentation sizes, using 10 augmentations as the baseline.

Additionally, training TimeGAN requires a substantial amount of data. Experiments conducted over different periods (5, 10, 15, and 20 years) showed that using 20 years of data for augmentation yielded the most robust results. This outcome is likely because the model identifies the generated data most similar to the testing period data from a large pool of candidates in the memory bank, necessitating the learning of long-term temporal patterns. Therefore, future studies should aim to develop methods that minimize the amount of data required for training augmentation models while maintaining high performance.

Moreover, future research could explore advanced predictive models that incorporate transformer-based methodologies, which are increasingly prominent in the AI field, in addition to CNN and LSTM architectures. The proposed model also holds potential for application in various financial markets, including foreign exchange, stocks, options, and real estate, offering avenues for further investigation.
