# Abstract

The creation of high fidelity synthetic data has long been an important goal in machine learning, particularly in fields like finance where the scarcityof available trainingand test data make it difficult to utilize many of the deep learning techniques which have proven so powerful in other domains.Despite ample research into different types of synthetic generation techniques,which in recent years have largely focused on generative adversarial networks, there remain key holes in many of the architectures and techniques being utilized.In particular, there are currently no techniques available which can generate multiple financial time series of returns concurrentlywhile capturing the specific statistical properties of financial time series and which incorporate extra information that influences the series,such as macroeconomic factors.We propose the Market Conditional Transformer-Encoder GenerativeAdversarialNetwork (MC-TE-GAN),a novel generative adversarial neural network architecture that satisfies the aforementioned challenges.When trained on daily data and compared with the benchmark CoMeTS-GAN model, MC-TE-GAN is able to more accurately capture the relevant univariate statistical properties of financial returns such as linear unpredictability and heteroskedasticity.It is also able to capture the multivariate correlations between concurrently generated series. Furthermore, we demonstrate that when incorporating macroeconomic data as conditioning information MC-TE-GAN works as a compelling historical scenario simulator which is able to create realistic synthetic market crashes or bull runs,even in unseen data.

# 1 Introduction

Deep learning has substantially changed the landscape of quantitative finance in recent years, but there are many confounding intricacies within financial markets which influence the adoption of this technology.For many fundamental financial problems the utilization of daily returns is relevant,but the short available histories for many assets at this time scale can limit its usefulness. Since particular assets have unique relationships with the market and the historical macroeconomic conditions which shape the environment surrounding the asset's valuation,it cannot always be assumed that behavior from one time series will generalize well when modeled and applied to another asset.Moreover, because there are a limited number of extreme market events in history (such as the 2008 financial crisis or the 2020 COVID-19 lockdown crash)and each one has unique dynamics at play influencing the pricing of assets,the relative importance for downstream tasks of the time series of particular assets (such as the S&P 500 or USD-JPY foreign exchange rate) in periods of abnormal market conditions is exceptionally high.

Following these observations,the generation of synthetic time series from multiple asset classes under specific macroeconomic conditions becomes a crucial area of research. Though it is possible to create semi-novel time series using traditional methods like bootstrapping,it is widely acknowledged that generative methods can create more diverse yet realistic datasets and are superior for making market scenario specific series that still offer a high degree of novelty [4,9].Deep neural networks in particular have gained popularity for the generation of these synthetic time series,particularly the Generative Adversarial Network architecture[5].The vast majority of the research done in this domain has either focused on univariate generation [10,22, 25],multivariate generation but within a single asset class or of strictly the correlation structure [12,24],or without any conditioning of the generation beyond the time series or statistics of the time series themselves [14].Rizzato et al.approach a similar problem by generating multi-asset equity portfolios conditioned on financial scenarios represented by instrument specific conditions and global macroeconomic features, but through modeling state transitions variables rather than the time series themselves [18].

When evaluating generated financial time series,the main univariate statistical properties that are important to capture are: lack ofauto-correlation of daily returns,excess kurtosis of the distribution of returns,volatility clustering,and the leverage effect [1].The main multivariate statistical property is the correlation structure between assets [14]; the varying interrelationship of time series dynamics is crucial for many portfolio diversification approaches and risk mitigation strategies [19].However, This correlation structure is not stationary,it changes under varying macroeconomic conditions,especially under extreme scenarios when the relationships between assets strengthen.Financial markets demonstrate tail dependence between the returns;when there is an extreme value in one variable the probability that another variable would also have an extreme value increases [2].

# 1.1Our contributions

In this paper,we introduce a solution to these problems: a novel macroeconomic conditional GAN architecture with a bespoke interior structure based on a transformer encoder designed to produce scenario dependent synthetic multi-asset class markets (MC-TEGAN). Our architecture is able to generate more realistic daily multi-asset markets when evaluated statistically in comparison to the benchmark.In addition, this paper has multiple unique contributions to the generation of synthetic financial markets beyond just this architecture,the multi-asset focus,and realism: we prove the usefulness of conditioning the generation on macroeconomic factors to achieve greater composability of the generated returns. We also developed a learning rate balancer for adversarial training, to prevent either the generator or discriminator from overpowering the other.Furthermore,we utilized the matching rate between assigned $k$ -meansorWasserstein $k$ -means clusters on the target and generated time series to determine the efficacy of our generation relative to conditioning.Lastly,we demonstrated a superior ability to simulate particular market scenarios such as crashes and greater responsiveness to changing macroeconomic environments when generating possible future paths.

# 2Related Work

There were multiple relevant approaches to the generation of synthetic time series which inspired our approach and established benchmarks for our generations.

Our work builds on Self-attention GANs,which were introduced in 2018 by Zhang et al. for use in image generation techniques [26]. Self-attention modules have earned widespread popularity in recent years in large scale language and vision modules partially because of their flexibility at learning representations that occur non-adjacently in the data. These are usually expressed as long term dependencies in time series data,but are spatial in images.In March of 2o22,Paul Jeha et al.[8] demonstrated the usefulness of self-attention in the context of time series generation with their paper: PSA-GAN: Progressive Self-Attention GANs for Synthetic Time Series.Though the focus of this paper was not explicitly financial time series,it introduced several key innovations that influenced our work.They utilized a relatively deeper architecture than other approaches,predicated on a main block with a spectral normalized convolutional layer and residual self-attention. The model also received time features as conditions like time of day,day of the year, day of the week, etc.which are relevant for the type of time series they were looking to generate.This model showed great results for simulating multiple different time series that were generally seasonal and related to load balancing (i.e. solar panel output and electricity usage). Self attention has been shown to be useful for financial time series: in particular by Lim etal.[11] and Eckerli et al. [4],among others.

An important work in the generation of multivariate markets, and the benchmark model we considered in this paper, was produced by Masi et al.when they introduced CoMeTS-GAN[14].In this paper, the authors generate equity mid-price log returns and volume data at a minute time scale for a universe of four and 30 stocks.Their architecture is based ona Conditional Wasserstein GAN that incorporated temporal convolutional layers,similar to those used in the QUANT GAN model [25],and spectral normalization to enforce the Lipschitz continuity constraint.A key innovation of their paper was passing the correlation structure of the generated and real data samples to the discriminator in conjunction with the time series data,guiding the generator to focus more directly on the correlation information.Their approach is quite successful at capturing the correlation dynamics of the multivariate markets,but they only condition their model on the past returns of each asset. They also partially ameliorate the data scarcity issue faced when using daily returns because they are using minute frequency data (for reference,in their research they utilized an order of magnitude more training data than is being used here).

# 3Market Conditional Transformer Encoder GAN

The architecture we developed took inspiration from the aforementioned self attention GANs and from transformer encoders which have seen great success in many sequence modeling tasks such as natural language processing as well as image related domains.

Though transformers often accept a tokenized input, in our case we passed the raw data to the encoder as we assumed the relatively small size of the training dataset made tokenization unnecessary or perhaps even deleterious.We recreated a transformer block styled on the original BERT[3] which is detailed in Fig.1,and then used a succession of these blocks for both the generator and discriminator. We tested multiple internal structural variations of blocks, including some using convolutional self attention and some using linear layers for the query,key,and value,but ultimately utilized linear layers.We used multiple blocks for both the generator and discriminator with the number of blocks being variable by the size of the market we were trying to generate (4 blocks for both generator and discriminator when generating 4 assets,6 blocks for the generator and 4 for the discriminator when generating 8 assets, etc.). In Fig.2 these hyper parameters are listed as $n _ { b \_ d }$ for the number of blocks for the discriminator and $n _ { b _ { - } g }$ for the generator.

For GANs, it is crucial to prevent mode collapse during training; this is generally considered as a motivation to use Wasserstein GANs.In practice,we found that utilizing spectral normalization with a regular discriminator was enough to prevent mode collapse [16].Because the Wasserstein loss compares the distributions of the synthetic and real data rather than the individual samples,itis often beneficial for generating more generic or broadly similar samples. However, since our interest was learning specific and unique market states,we chose to progress witha regular discriminator/BCE loss in conjunction with spectral normalized weights rather than using Wasserstein loss like CoMeTS-GAN or Conditional Sig-Wasserstein GAN[10].

We also built a learning rate stabilizer to manage the training of our networks rather than setting a learning rate schedule a priori.The stabilizer takes a measurement of the loss values for the generator and the discriminator every ten epochs,and determines if they are imbalanced in favor of either network,which we define as $L ^ { g } < = ( L ^ { d } * 0 . 9 )$ or $L ^ { g } > = ( L ^ { d } * 1 0 )$ . If the networks are unbalanced,the stabilizer records a tally in favor of the losing network.If the tally of either network makes it to a particular level set as a hyperparameter (usually 5,corresponding to 50 epochs of unbalanced training), the learning rate of the winning network is lightly penalized and the learning rate of the losing network is boosted by the equivalent amount.This did not lead to substantially different training results than manual monitoring of the training of the network; the sets of adversarial networks converged similarly when regulated bya human observer or by the stabilizer.However, it did prevent degenerate training where one network overpowered the other,and made it easier to train without manual intervention. This technique is similar to the more formalized balancer detailed by Hazimeh et al., though ours was designed independently from their paper [6].

As shown in Fig.2, the MC-TE-GAN system accepts as input to the generator a set of features (which can either be macroeconomic as discussed in this paper,or company-specific features such as fundamental data) of shape $n _ { a } \times n _ { f }$ where $n _ { a }$ is the number of assets and $n _ { f }$ is the number of features,and the past time series of each of the assets under consideration of shape $n _ { a } \times P$ where $P$ denotes the number of prior series values to consider (i.e.the look-back period).A noise vector of size $n _ { a } \times Z$ is then concatenated onto the conditions,and the whole matrix of shape $n _ { a } \times \left( n _ { f } + P + Z \right)$ is then passed to the neural network.The generator contains a linear layer that scales the input to the hidden size of the network where it is then passed to the series of transformer blocks.Then a sequence of two linear layers gradually scales this output back to the output size corresponding to the look-forward period,i.e.the number of future series variables we want to consider: $F$ .The matrix of generated future series or real future series from the historical data is then appended onto the matrix of conditioning features and prior series, such that a matrix of shape $n _ { a } \times \left( n _ { f } + P + F \right)$ is formed.These real and synthetic samples with their associated conditions are then passed to the discriminator which outputs a predicted class label for each example in a traditional GAN training setup.

# 4.1 Preprocessing and Pipelining

Regardless of which dataset we passed through our network, we had a consistent pipeline for processing and preparing_the data for training.Firstlywetransfomedthepriceata $\{ P _ { t } \} _ { t = 0 } ^ { T }$ for each financial instrument into returns by taking the percent change between the daily close of each instrument before taking the log of those returns.

$$
R _ { t } = \log \left( \frac { P _ { t } } { P _ { t - 1 } } \right) , \quad \mathrm { f o r } t = 1 , 2 , . . . , T
$$

Secondly,we min-max scaled the data being passed to the network in the following manner:

$$
\tilde { R } _ { t } = 2 \cdot \frac { R _ { t } - \underset { 1 \leq s \leq t } { \operatorname* { m i n } } R _ { s } } { \underset { 1 \leq s \leq t } { \operatorname* { m a x } } R _ { s } - \underset { 1 \leq s \leq t } { \operatorname* { m i n } } R _ { s } } - 1 , \quad \mathrm { f o r } t = 1 , 2 , . . . , T
$$

This scaling maps the returns $R _ { t }$ to the range $( - 1 , 1 )$ using the minimum and maximum returns in an expanding window up to time $t$ ,thusavoiding look-ahead bias.Thirdly,we partitioned the data into discrete samples of the generation window size from a continuous set of time series.More specifically,we create a new generation window starting at each day in our series. Assuming a day $t$ as the pivot of our sample splitting, we took the preceding sixty-four days $( t - 6 3 : t )$ of returns for each series as the prior series for conditioning:

$$
x _ { - } s _ { \mathrm { p r i o r } } ( t ) = \left( \tilde { R } _ { t - 6 3 } , \tilde { R } _ { t - 6 2 } , \ldots , \tilde { R } _ { t } \right) , \quad \mathrm { f o r } t = 6 3 , 6 4 , \ldots , T - 6 4 
$$

Then we took the subsequent sixty-four days $( t + 1 : t + 6 4 )$ as the target series:

$$
x \_ s _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } } ( t ) = \left( \tilde { R } _ { t + 1 } , \tilde { R } _ { t + 2 } , \ldots , \tilde { R } _ { t + 6 4 } \right) , \quad \mathrm { f o r } t = 6 3 , 6 4 , \ldots , T - 6 4
$$

In regards to the macroeconomic conditioning values,we took a sample of just the values at day t. So when generating data, the generator took a tensor of latent variables sampled from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and unit variance,the prior sixty-four day return window,and optionally the macroeconomic conditioning variables.

For our training data with four series we had a total of 6060 days starting in January,1997and ending in January of 2021 when training and testing fully in-sample (IS).When considering out-ofsample (OOS) training and test splits,the training data had 5632 days ending in August of 2o19,and the testing data contained 273 days starting on January 1st, 202o (the input sequences extended backwards 64 observations into the past but those days were not evaluated for generation).When training with eight or sixteen assets,we had a total of 4286 days,starting in February of 2003 for fully in-sample,and the equivalent dates considering the changed series start date for the COVID-19 OOS scenario train /test splits.

# 4.2 Training and Model Specifications

The training regime for the MC-TE-GAN was relatively consistent between the different generations.We initialized our generator with a dropout rate of O.1,and a learning rate of $4 e - 4$ Our discriminator had a initial dropout of 0.25 and learning rate of $4 e - 6$ As mentioned in the prior section about our adversarial training balancer, the learning rates were not consistent throughout the whole training but rather dynamically adjusted.We trained the model for a variable number of epochs,and determined a stopping point by measuring per series performance against multiple statistical tests designed to determine whether two sets of data come from the same distribution: the Wilcoxon test and the Mann-Whitney U test.The training usually lasted between 1200-1500 epochs for the MC-TE-GAN with macroeconomic conditioning and 800-1000 epochs without.When training our benchmark, CoMeTS-GAN, we followed their recommended hyperparameters with few exceptions. We changed the length of the input conditioning time series and generated output to match the data format described in the prior section.We also tested various numbers of epochs (three hundred, one thousand,and one thousand five hundred) for optimal generation,butultimately determined that there was no benefit to training longer than their recommended three hundred epochs. One clear strength of the CoMeTS-GAN was its relative speed and stability in training-even without a training balancer, we did not experience any truly degenerate training runs where one of the models completely defeated the other.Furthermore,CoMeTS-GAN took around one third the training time per epoch of the MC-TE-GAN with macroeconomic conditioning.In situations where the fastest trainingis absolutely essentialor for the GPU-poor, CoMeTS-GAN is likely the superior choice.

# 4.3 Generation Strategy

Though our model was capable of generating arbitrarily long sequences autoregressively,we chose to evaluate the performance on series of the exact generated series length (i.e.sixty-four days). Since our aim is specifically to create a macroeconomic scenario specific generator, we were not as concerned with generating arbitrarily long sequences but rather the specific subset of days which encompass large market crashes like the COVID-19 lockdown market crash or the 2oo8 financial crisis.When performing our scenario tests,we generate ten paths from the start day $t$ ,each witha different latent vector.

# 4.4 Cluster Matching for Determining Conditional Fit

One of the challenges of generating conditional time series data is validating the responsiveness of the generation to the conditions. Models can often learn to generate globally statistically accurate data, for example conserving the relevant stylized facts or within the distribution of the broader dataset, without truly learning to distinguish strongly between the conditional states.This distinction makes it necessary to test conditioned generative data in its relationship to the real observed statewhich emerged relative to the conditions: the conditionally realized data.This analysis is complicated by the inherently noisy nature of financial time series,there are alarge number of theoretically plausible time series which could proceed from a given conditional state.If there is a large number of theoretically acceptable generations, then making a direct comparison between the real target series and the generated series is sub-optimal; this would reward a model which simply learned to memorize the data and thus was not generalizable or capable of creating multiple novel but plausible future paths relative to a given condition.In order to test this exact ability and achieve the end of validating conditional generation with a measure specifically compares the similarity of samples with common conditions, we devised a test based on matching clusters between generated and synthetic samples.The most general formulation of the cluster matching validation approach can be implemented using any clustering algorithm. Given the target time series windows and the equivalent generated times series corresponding to the same days,

$$
\begin{array} { r } { W _ { \mathrm { r e a l , t } } = x \mathrm { _ - } s _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t , t } } , \quad t = 6 3 , \ldots , T - 6 4 } \\ { W _ { \mathrm { g e n , t } } = \hat { x } \mathrm { _ - } s _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t , t } } , \quad t = 6 3 , \ldots , T - 6 4 } \end{array}
$$

we concatenate the generated windows onto the set of real windows, such that we have an equal number of generated and real series correspondingto discrete days.This forms the combined dataset for clustering:

$$
\mathcal { W } = \{ W _ { \mathrm { r e a l } , t } \} \cup \{ W _ { \mathrm { g e n } , t } \} , \quad t = 6 3 , . . . , T - 6 4
$$

With this dataset, we apply a clustering algorithm $C$ to all windows in $\mathcal { W }$

$$
c _ { W } = C ( W ) , \quad \forall W \in \mathcal { W }
$$

Which gives us a cluster assignment for each of the real and generated windows:

$$
\begin{array} { r } { c _ { \mathrm { r e a l } , t } = C \left( W _ { \mathrm { r e a l } , t } \right) } \\ { c _ { \mathrm { g e n } , t } = C \left( W _ { \mathrm { g e n } , t } \right) } \end{array}
$$

Lastly, we compare cluster assgnments and compute accuracy:

$$
{ \mathrm { A c c u r a c y } } = \left( { \frac { 1 } { T - 1 2 8 } } \sum _ { t = 6 3 } ^ { T - 6 4 } \delta \left( c _ { \mathrm { r e a l } , t } , c _ { \mathrm { g e n } , t } \right) \right) \times 1 0 0 \%
$$

where $\delta ( a , b )$ is the Kronecker delta function:

$$
\delta ( a , b ) = { \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { 1 , } & { { \mathrm { i f ~ } } a = b } \\ { 0 , } & { { \mathrm { i f ~ } } a \neq b } \end{array} \right. }
$$

In the univariate clustering case, we proceed as above for each of the individual series,and then calculate the overall matching accuracy relative to all samples in each series.In the true multivariate clustering case,we apply the relevant clustering algorithm to all of the assets together.

In our research,we chose to use $k$ -means as a baseline clustering method because it is a standard,simple,and well understood algorithm which has been validated for consistency on the task of daily return clustering [13].We also utilized Wasserstein- $k$ -means (WK-means) which was shown by Horvath et al.[7] to be more effective than other common approaches for regime clustering on univariate log-return series.WK-means was further validated for multivariate regime clusteringbyMc Greevy etal.[15].We present results for multivariate clustering with both approaches,but for univariate cases we only utilize standard $k$ -means. Our primary motivation for this was compute time oriented - the Wasserstein distance calculations are relatively slow: the $k$ -means clusters can be computed in seconds on our time series data whereas WK-means can take hours depending on dataset size.Furthermore,many of the known limitations of the $k$ -means algorithm are also not relevant in our case - our data is already scaled appropriately, the variance of log returns is not generally so high as to create extreme outliers (relative to the tolerance of the algorithm) over long periods of time, and the matching of clusters between the real and synthetic data can still distinguish similar series even if the exactly optimal $k$ has not been found.To demonstrate the discriminative ability of our clusters,we exhibit in Fig.3 the average returns of clusters found only on the real S&P 5o0 returns- it is clear that individual clusters display distinctive patterns in aggregate like a general upwards or downwards trend.Since the majority of the history of the $\mathrm { S } \& \mathrm { P } \ 5 0 0$ is bullish,the three cluster average returns are all positive,but this is not necessarily the case for all of the time series we considered like currency pairs.In addition,as the number of clusters increases and smaller outlier clusters are formed,the difficulty of cluster matching increases substantially. Since we cluster all of the real and generated data as one set of inputs,if the generated data is sufficiently divergent from the real data then the clustering algorithm can assign a cluster to the generated data alone thus negating some of the possibility of random matches.

# 5Results

In this section,we present the results of our experiments by their statistical properties and cluster matching performance in multiple scenarios.We look at multivariate generation under two conditions: fully in-sample generation due to the small number of samples available when utilizing daily data,and out-of-sample generation rolling forward from the end of 2019 to the beginning of 2021.We demonstrate the ability of MC-TE-GAN to more accurately capture the general statistical properties of the market in low data availability scenarios,while being more responsive to changing economic conditions,in comparison to the CoMeTS-GAN benchmark.

# 5.1 Cluster Matching and Statistical Properties

We compared the cluster matching percentages for the generations from MC-TE-GANand our benchmark model CoMeTS-GAN; MCTE-GAN was superior across every generation and dataset which can be seen in Tab.3 and Tab.4.To make a more direct comparison between the ability of each model to learn strictly from time series, we also trained the MC-TE-GAN without any macroeconomic conditioning variables (W/M) and it still outperformed the benchmark CoMeTS-GAN, though broadly underperformed the MC-TE-GAN with macroeconomic conditioning (W M).The MC-TE-GAN(W M) nearly sweeps the three cluster tests,as well as performs the best on the ten cluster tests - indicating a strong ability to learn unique generative functions for various macroeconomic states that it has seen previously.This bodes well for its ability to create realistic historical simulations as shown in Fig.7.When testing the univariate cluster matching out-of-sample,the series generated by MC-TE-GAN (W/M) mostly show the best performance.This is likely due to the decreased generation diversity from the base MC-TE-GAN compared to the version with macro conditioning - without the macro conditions the model tends to create realistic butfairly standard time series that tend to have smaller directional trends and less unique paths,and thus are more likely to be closer to unseen data.However, this comes with a downside in the sense that the model is less composable in regards to changing economic conditions.The model with macroeconomic conditioning seems to be better at generating markets that mirror the directional trends or volatility conditions seen in the realized market; we demonstrate this in greater detail in the following section.

![](images/f2b88dea520a925bd08b1b201ee1ff453e40f56a7bb8ba60abccb79a8799d45d.jpg)  
Fig.4: The in-sample autocorrelation for all of the real and generated series.

When looking at in-sample statistical properties,we are able to accurately model the observed properties ina reliable manner. In some of the plots,we only show results from the MC-TE-GAN with macroeconomic conditioning since this is our primary interest and because in those cases the properties of the two MC-TE-GAN derived series were not substantially divergent.In Fig.4,both models capture a similarly low level of autocorrelation.In a few instances, the MC-TE-GAN series seem to follow the oscillating pattern of autocorrelation between lags in the real series,but this effect is too inconsistent to be reliably attributed to anything beyond chance. The autocorrelation of the CoMeTS-GAN series is nearly the same for each series which stands in contrast to the individualistic structures found in the real market.In Fig.5,both models capture the decreasing autocorrelation of volatility with time,with the exception of the small increase in GSCI autocorrelation of volatility after 5 lagged days.In terms of distance from the properties of the real series,MC-TE-GAN seems to be closer and thus more realistic.The correlation structures between the assets displayed in Fig. 6,ostensibly the strength of the CoMeTS-GAN model,also favors the generations from MC-TE-GAN. Though neither model accurately captures the exact structure of the real market, the magnitude of the correlations of the MC-TE-GAN generated series is far closer to the real market.Furthermore,the CoMeTS-GANconsistently overestimates the correlation between the SPX and the USD-JPY pair as well as between the USDT-5Y series and the GSCI series - this same behavior was seen in the COVID-19 scenario test even though the training and testing data covers different subsets of days. We only report some of the statistical results from the in-sample tests for brevity,but in general they were not substantially different in the out-of-sample testing.

# 5.2Modeling the COVID-19 Crisis

We chose to test the out-of-sample abilities of the models specifically during the COVID-19 crisis because it posed a relatively challenging and unique paradigm of market dynamics including a true equity price crash,as well as a substantial amount of preceding data for training.Market crash scenarios are particularly valuable as strategy testing data or as synthetic training data for data augmentation,yet there are few examples in modern financial history, making the COVID-19 scenario especially compelling.We compare here again the performance of our model against the CoMeTS-GAN model as a benchmark.

# 6Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we demonstrated the performance of a novel GAN architecture,MC-TE-GAN,for generating multi-asset financial markets with conditioning based on macroeconomic features.

When modeling daily returns,our architecture demonstrates superior performance relative to comparable architectures. On top of our contribution of a novel architecture,we also introduced an adversarial training balancer to stabilize GAN training and a technique for determining the efficacy of conditional generation using cluster matching.We also validated the efficacy of our generation by confirming the univariate and multivariate stylized facts,as well as examining the realistic diversity of generation under specific market crash scenarios.

![](images/72f728eb14bb1e379658423f7c081149e4dc8799db2de25ba07d9f9558af9fd4.jpg)  
Fig.10: Comparison between multiple generations of 64 days from the same start date (April 24th, 2020) during the equity bull market recovery after the COVID-19 lockdown stock market crash.

There are multiple avenues to extend on this research in the future. One of the lowest hanging fruit is adding more conditioning data to the model with additional fine grained macroeconomic features and asset specific features that describe their financial state or the recent history of the time series.Our architecture is in fact built to accept individual per asset features and to generate conditioning features though we did not report those capabilities in this paper. More information regarding the performance of MC-TE-GAN in other scenarios,with more generated assets,and in comparison to more traditional data generation techniques such as GARCH can be found in our earlier publication [2o].In terms of architecture, many other researchers in the space of generative modeling for financial time series have pointed to the potential of diffusion models and we echo this optimism.In regards to conditional diffusion relative to macroeconomic features,building a joint embedding model (similar to the CLIP model[17] which guides generation in text-to-image diffusion models) for macroeconomic features and market returns could offer even more granular control over the generated series than what is demonstrated in this paper- especially if combined with cross attention.Furthermore,we could potentially extend the performance of our transformer-based architecture by introducing tokenization and embedding of the time series - perhaps with a similar strategy to wav2vec [21] or TOTEM[23].This would more easily allow variable generation size in terms of number of assets or length of series,while also potentially opening up the ability for the model to learn more generalizable financial market behavior from far more data - something that is crucially important in our daily return setting where data availability is comparatively weaker than other similar problems.These specific research directions all offer tantalizing prospects in addition to the many other potential avenues for building off of this research in terms of architecture, dataset curation,scenario analysis,and downstream use-cases for the generated data.
