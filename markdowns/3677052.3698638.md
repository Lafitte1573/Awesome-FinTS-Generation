# Abstract

We propose a data-driven model called conditional time-series generative adversarial networks (CTS-GAN),for simulating financial time-series data, like asset prices.While GANs have been applied to data generation, existing models struggle to capture the temporal characteristics of time-series data.TimeGAN,a GAN-variant, addressed this by incorporating a learned latent space jointly optimized with supervised and adversarial goals for in-sample data generation.In finance however, practical applications often require out-of-sample time-series data,simulated under specific conditions. We thus implement our five-network architecture and loss functions with allowance for conditional information,in order to generate out-of-sample multivariate time-series data.Evaluations conducted on the generated data illustrates the high quality of CTS-GAN's outputs,and a potential application in risk management for stock-only portfolios is demonstrated.

# 1 Introduction

Traditionally,statistical models like the autoregressive (AR) or the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (GARCH) and their variants [29] have been developed and used for simulation of time-series data.Specifically, the simulation of asset price data is an exceptionally difficult endeavor due to the complexity of financial markets [3,30],which not only requires accurate, precise and timely modelling,but one that is also heavily dependent on the assumption of the underlying data distribution.With the advent of deep learning,generative methods offer to address such challenges via data-driven,i.e.model-free methods.Generative adversarial networks (GANs) is one such example that can generate data based only on the sampled real data,with no restrictions on input or assumptions [14]. GANs are made up of two neural networks, namely the generator $( G )$ and discriminator $( D )$ ,which engage in a two-player min-max game.

Though the concept of GAN was insightful on its own, the true potential of the framework was unlocked when improvements and variants of GANs were developed.These can be broadlyclassified into two types,namely improvements to the loss function and improvements to the architectures.Improvements made to the loss function were made to introduce attractive mathematical properties to enable the adversarial training process to achieve a more stable,convergent performance.An incisive example would be the replacement of the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence in GANs,with the Wasserstein metric in Wasserstein GANs (WGAN)[1].On the other hand, improvements made to the architecture were made mostly to deal with the types of data the user was dealing with, e.g. deep convolutional GANs (DCGAN) [27] that introduced the use of convolutional layers within $G$ and $D$ in order to process image data better, or conditional GANs (CGAN) [22] which allowed categorical or numerical labels to be passed to the $G$ and $D$ ,according to user specification.

GANs dealing with time-series data requires us to invoke recurrent architectures in $G$ and $D$ ,to deal with time dependency [23]. Often, we break the temporal dependence by differencing the data, e.g.working with the asset returns instead of asset prices,which fits well with both the vanilla multilayer perceptron (MLP) type architectures and loss functions.Another way to treat time-series data would be to assume that sequences of data are independent and identically-distributed (i.i.d.).That is the route taken in timeseries GAN (TimeGAN) [36],and we believe this to be the parsimonious middle ground between the assumption of pre-processed i.i.d.data at every time-step versus a complicated inter-temporal, inter-dependent model.TimeGAN,however,would turn out to be insufficient as the user is unable to control or place conditions on the inputs to the model,which is imperative in finance.Another limitation is that TimeGAN generates data that is in-sample,i.e. architecturally, the model is generating data for time-steps that have already been realized,which reduces the scope of its use in finance,as forward-looking,out-of-sample data drives financial decision-making.

With sight of the limitations of generating time-series data given, we develop a model, namely conditional time-series GAN (CTS-GAN)by revisiting TimeGAN and restructuring the architecture to handle multivariate conditional data,using concepts found in CGAN.We then modify the loss functions to handle the new changes made to the architecture. Crucially, we introduce a moving window approach [26] to data generation that allows for out-ofsample data generation,as it allows us to generate as small as one time-step ahead to as long as one requires,so long as up-to-date data is available.Following which,we check the quality of the simulated data using stylized facts for both univariate and multivariate data,before applying the model to a real-world example found in risk management.The data and implementation codes in Python can be found here: https://github.com/riasatalistiaque/cts-gan.

# 1.1 Related Works

Generation of financial time-series data is a well-studied problem and closely related to the problem of forecasting,which is why early attempts at generating such data were done by repurposing tools developed from model-based econometric approaches [4].A survey of the modern machinery used to generate synthetic data involves the use of deep learning techniques in an attempt to be model-free.We look at some related works that have attempted to generate financial time-series data.

Adversarial-based approaches preserve the GAN framework of trying to optimize a loss function via adversarial training.Developed around the same time as TimeGAN, Quant GAN [33] retained the two-network framework but made an adjustment to the network architecture to handle temporal data via convolutions.This was made specially to handle financial data. Over in the field of forecasting,ForGAN[17] incorporated conditional information while retaining the two-network framework,for one time-step ahead prediction,which meant that once the prediction was realized, it was imperative to check the difference between the prediction and the realizations.Their focus was not specifically for finance,but the conceptual framework was created out of the awareness of the difference in how realized data was perceived to be in-sample. Fin-GAN[32] introduced a novel loss function to the adversarial framework,while the modified CTGAN method [25],utilized a Tabular GAN [35],which is a type of GAN specifically designed to handle the unique challenges that arise in tabular data, including mixed discrete and continuous variables,and imbalanced classes, to generate financial time-series data for the purposes of portfolio optimization with constraints.

It is also worth mentioning that encoder-decoder-based [31], flow-based [34],signature-based [20],and transformer-based [19] approaches have also been explored in attempting to generate financial time-series data. Our paper focuses on analysing the quality of the generated out-of-sample synthetic financial time-series data of CTS-GAN,with scope for future works,outlined in Section 4.1.

# 2Methodology

# 2.1 Dataset

In order to obtain a set of publicly accessible multivariate timeseries data,specifically the daily adjusted close prices of assets,we looked at stocks in the S&P5oo,broken down by sectors and ranked by within-sector weights.We then selected five,i.e. $q = 5$ ,stocks in the following manner: two stocks from the communication services sector,Meta Platforms,Inc.(META) and Alphabet Inc.(GOOG); two stocks from the technology sector:Apple, Inc.(AAPL) and Microsoft Corporation (MSFT),and one stock from the consumer discretionary sector: Amazon.com,Inc.(AMZN).The time-series data runs from18 May 2012 to 27 October 2023,fora total of 2880 data points.This covers most of the recent economic history, from the major bull run post Global Financial Crisis of 2oo8 [11],up until the unprecedented COVID-19 drawdown in 2020.

# 2.2Moving Window Approach

We deploy a moving window approach for our model as encapsulated by Figure 1.By seting the window length $l _ { w } = 2 5 7$ ,we get a total of 2623 out-of-sample windows.We treat the first window of 257 prices to have been realized and compute the one-day differences followed by the normalization of the data within the window. We then breakup the window into a collection of sequences of length $l _ { s } = 1 2$ days in order to obtain a collection of 245 sequences. As specified in Section 1.2,we further split the collection of sequences into two parts, the conditional c and the unconditional $\mathbf { x }$ parts,both of which would then be used for training CTS-GAN. In order to expedite the learning process,for the following window we initialize the networks’parameters using the previous days' learned networks’weights.

The one-day offset procedure,used post-training,of the conditional data from the final sequence in order to generate data whose final value would be one time-step outside of the window,can be amended to handle offsets of up to $l _ { s } - l _ { c }$ .However, we note that the conditional data must be realized,i.e.exist within that window, in order to prevent leakage.This means that while we look at only one time-step out-of-sample in this study,one can easily amend the CTS-GAN architecture to handle the generation of many time-steps out-of-sample,if necessary.

# 2.3 Training

GANs engage in a two-player game, as defined by a min-max loss function as:

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { G } \operatorname* { m a x } _ { D } \mathbf { \Pi } _ { \mathbf { x } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { X } } [ \log D ( \mathbf { x } ) ] + \operatorname* { \mathbb { E } } _ { \mathbf { z } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { Z } } } [ \log \left( 1 - D ( G ( \mathbf { z } ) ) \right) ] ,
$$

where $G$ tries to generate data that exhibits characteristics that are similar to the real data $\mathbf { x }$ such that $D$ is unable to discriminate between them. CGANs,which appeared shortlyafter GANs, amended the architecture such that additional information could be passed to both $G$ and $D$ so that the training and generation could be controllable via user-defined inputs.The loss function:

$$
\underset { G } { \mathop { \operatorname* { m i n } } } \underset { D } { \mathop { \operatorname* { m a x } } } \underset { { \mathbf { x } } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \chi } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log D ( { \mathbf { x } } ; { \mathbf { c } } ) ] + \underset { { \mathbf { z } } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { Z } } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log \left( 1 - D ( G ( { \mathbf { z } } ; { \mathbf { c } } ) ; { \mathbf { c } } ) \right) ] ,
$$

has no change other than indicating that both $G$ and $D$ would accept the condition labels $\mathbf { c }$

CTS-GAN comprises of five neural networks, namely the embedder $( E )$ ,recovery (R)and supervisor (S),in addition to $G$ and $D$ all of which are trained across three phases.The training scheme is provided in Figure 2.In the first phase, $\mathbf { x } .$ ，which exists in the feature space, $\chi$ ,is embedded into the latent space $\mathcal { H }$ via $E$ .We thus train $E$ and $R$ simultaneously to minimize the reconstruction loss function:

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { R } : = | | \tilde { \mathbf { x } } - \mathbf { x } | | _ { 2 } , \mathrm { w h e r e } \ \mathbf { h } = E ( \mathbf { x } ) , \tilde { \mathbf { x } } = R ( \mathbf { h } ) ,
$$

which captures the ability of $E$ to embed, and $R$ to recover $\mathbf { x } \in { \mathcal { X } }$ as highlighted by flow number 1 in Figure 2.In the second phase, with $E$ and $R$ trained,we then have to learn the temporal dynamics of $\mathbf { x }$ in the latent space,which is done via S.We embed $\mathbf { x }$ via the trained $E$ then pass it through $S$ and then minimize the temporal loss function:

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { T } : = | | \mathbf { h } _ { s } - \mathbf { h } | | _ { 2 } , \mathrm { ~ w h e r e ~ } \mathbf { h } _ { s } = S ( \mathbf { h } ) ,
$$

which captures the ability of $S$ to learn the temporal dynamics given $\mathbf { h }$ .This is highlighted by flow number 2in Figure 2.In the final phase,with $E , R$ and S trained, we now use the CGAN setup in order to train $G$ and $D , D$ is trying to distinguish between the real and generated conditional data via the min-max,sum of two loss function:

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { G } { \operatorname* { m a x } _ { D } { \beta \mathcal { L } _ { U } } } + \mathcal { L } _ { S } + \gamma | | \hat { \mathbf { x } } - \mathbf { x } | | _ { 2 } ,
$$

namely the unsupervised and the supervised loss functions:

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { U } : = \underset { { \bf h } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { H } } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log D ( { \bf h } ; { \bf c } ) ] + \underset { { \bf z } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { Z } } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log \left( 1 - D ( G ( { \bf z } ; { \bf c } ) ; { \bf c } ) \right) ] ,
$$

$$
\mathcal { L } _ { S } : = \underset { \mathbf { h } _ { s } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { H } } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log D ( \mathbf { h } _ { s } ; \mathbf { c } ) ] + \underset { \mathbf { z } \sim \mathbb { P } _ { \mathcal { Z } } } { \mathbb { E } } [ \log \left( 1 - D ( S ( G ( \mathbf { z } ; \mathbf { c } ) ) ; \mathbf { c } ) \right) ] ,
$$

respectively with a regularization term used to ensure that the generated data exhibits similar first and second moments of the real data:

$$
\hat { \mathbf { x } } = R ( S ( G ( \mathbf { z } ; \mathbf { c } ) ) ) .
$$

The terms $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are regularization parameters,aimed at stabilizing training. Under the unsupervised setting, $G$ is trying to generate data which mimics the atemporal characteristics of $\mathbf { x }$ in a way that, once embedded, $D$ is unable to discriminate between them.Furthermore,once the embedded data passes through the supervisor, under the supervised setting,picking up the temporal characteristics, $D$ should be fooled into treating the generated data as nothing but real data from $\chi$ . Training of $G$ is highlighted by flows 3 and 4, while training of $D$ is highlighted by flows 3 and 6 for the unsupervised loss and flows 4 and 7 for the supervised loss, with the regularization term is represented by flow 5 in Figure 2.

Once training is complete,we obtain a batch of noise $\mathbf { z }$ from $z$ and the condition c on which we wish to generate the data,and pass it through the generator into the latent space. We then pass that through the supervisor to learn the temporal characteristics and then finally through the recovery network in order to obtain a sequence $\hat { \mathbf { x } } \in \mathcal { X }$ (8).Note that post-processing by reversing the normalization process and one-day differencing has to be done,in order to actually obtain a price sequence.

# 2.4Implementation

The algorithm pseudocode is provided in Algorithm 1. Following Section 2.2,we set the window length $l _ { w } = 2 5 7$ ,thus giving us the number of out-of-sample windows $n _ { w } = 2 6 2 3$ ,as we use the moving window approach. Within every window, we set the sequence length $l _ { s } = 1 2$ and conditional length $l _ { c } = 7$ to obtain a collection of 245 sequences from which we draw a batch of sequences of size $m = 3 2$ for every iteration across $n _ { i } = 2 0 0 0$ iterations.With the sequence length set at $l _ { s } = 1 2$ and $l _ { c } = 7$ ,we require a sequential noise space $z$ of length $l _ { s } - l _ { c } = 5$ to sample from for $G$ .Whilea Wiener process for $z$ was used in TimeGAN [36],as it relates to asequence-related deep learning task,we opted to use a uniform distribution as we found it to be just as performant.It has the same dimension as the feature space $q = 5$ In order to incorporate conditional information, we take $\mathbf { c } ^ { ( j ) }$ and flatten it into a 1d-vector and repeat it $l _ { s } - l _ { c }$ times.We then concatenate this tensor with the noise space,and the latent space tensors as inputs for $G$ (lines 26 and 35), and $D$ respectively.

# 3.2Visualizations

As we are dealing with multivariate data with $k > 2$ ,we have to consider dimensionality-reduction methods when trying to visualizeand compare between the structures of the realized data to that of the generated data.We use two such methods in order to plot the results in Figure 5,namely the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding(t-SNE)method [15] for the simulated out-of-sample asset prices that were collated,and the principal components analysis (PCA) method for the simulated out-of-sample asset returns computed from those prices.The plots corroborate what has been iterated in Section 3.1,in that the data that is generated should mind the distribution of the real data that it is trying to mimic.From the PCA plot,we can not only observe that the generated reduced data aligns well with the mode of the real reduced data,but that the generated reduced data also shows diversity along the axis of the scattered representations of the simulated returns.While useful, these visualizations are well-known to be heuristic in nature [2], and should only be used as a visual aid.

# 3.3Stylized Facts

A better approach would be to check if the simulated out-of-sample asset prices obey stylized facts that are empirically observed across many assets,markets and time-scales.Instead of checking at the individual asset-level, we again construct EW portfolios.We show that CTS-GAN generated data corroborates with the following stylized facts.

3.3.1Absence of Linear Autocorrelation.A widely known property of empirical asset returns [6,12],is the absence of linear autocorrelation for time-lags $\geq 1 5$ minutes [8].

$$

$$

3.3.2Heavy-Tailed Distribution.Studies [6,21] have showed that the empirical distribution of asset returns should be modelled by a heavy-tailed distribution as the returns'distribution fall much slower as compared to that of a normal distribution.The probability distribution $P ( r )$ is empirically known to consistently follow a power-law decay in the tails:

$$
P ( r ) \propto r ^ { - \beta } ,
$$

where the value of the fitted $\beta$ is typically between 3 to 5 [30].We found this value to be 4.69 for simulated data,and 3.79 for realized data.Both the realized and simulated portfolio returns exhibit a heavy-tailed distribution,as shown in Figure 7,where extreme returns occur with a higher probability.

3.3.3Volatility Clustering.Volatility is the long-range slow-decay of autocorrelation of returns which was shown to remain significantly positive over some period of time [7]:

$$
C _ { \nu } ( k ) = c o r r ( | r _ { t } | ^ { \nu } , | r _ { t + k } | ^ { \nu } ) .
$$

We used $\nu \ = \ 1$ as the correlation was found to be highest for this value [10],given any time-lag $k$ .For time-lags $\le ~ 1 0 0$ days, this autocorrelation for both the realized and simulated portfolio returns exhibits a power-law decay [30] which implies a long-range temporal dependence in the portfolio returns,as shown in Figure 8.

3.3.4Coarse-Fine Volatility Correlation.The coarse-fine volatility correlation is a multi-time-scale analysis of volatility [24].The coarse volatility $v _ { c } ^ { \tau }$ is the absolute value of the sum of the returns in $\tau$ trading days and fine volatility $v _ { f } ^ { \tau }$ is the sum of the absolute value of the returns in $\tau$ trading days, given by:

$$
v _ { c } ^ { \tau } ( t ) = \left| \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { \tau } r _ { t - i } \right| \quad \mathrm { a n d } \quad v _ { f } ^ { \tau } ( t ) = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { \tau } | r _ { t - i } | ,
$$

respectively.We chose $\tau = 5$ as it stands for day-week time-scales [30].The temporal interactions of the two different time-scales is analysed [24] using the lead-lag correlation function (13).The difference of the lead-lag correlation:

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \Delta \rho _ { c f } ^ { \tau } ( k ) = \rho _ { c f } ^ { \tau } ( k ) - \rho _ { c f } ^ { \tau } ( - k ) , } \end{array}
$$

is computed as well, where:

$$
\rho _ { c f } ^ { \tau } ( k ) = c o r r ( v _ { c } ^ { \tau } ( t + k ) , v _ { f } ^ { \tau } ( t ) ) .
$$

The negative asymmetry, quantified by the difference,indicates that coarse volatility has the power to predict fine volatility [13, 24]. This is consistent with the assumption that volatility is created when different market agents have different time horizons and are likely to settle for different prices across different market situations. In both the realized and simulated portfolios,the coarse volatility has the power to predict fine volatility,as shown in Figure 9.

3.3.5Gain/Loss Asymmetry. The distribution of the investment horizon, which is defined as:

$$
\begin{array} { r } { T _ { w a i t } ^ { t } ( \theta ) = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { \operatorname* { i n f } \{ k : \log \dot { p } _ { t + k } - \log \dot { p } _ { t } \geq \theta \} , } & { \theta > 0 , } \\ { \operatorname* { i n f } \{ k : \log \dot { p } _ { t + k } - \log \dot { p } _ { t } \leq \theta \} , } & { \theta < 0 , } \end{array} \right. } \end{array}
$$

i.e. the waiting time $k > 0$ ,needed to reach a fixed level of return for the first time,from time $t$ [16],fora certain $\pm \theta$ is different.It is observed that draw-downs are faster than draw-ups,with the peak of positive returns coming after the peak of negative returns, indicating the presence of asymmetry in price change [16].This is clearly exhibited by both the realized and simulated portfolios as the peak of positive returns visibly comes after the peak of negative returns,as shown in Figure 10.

3.3.6Marchenko-Pastur Distribution.We now look at the correlation and covariance matrices computed from the underlying asset returns, for a multivariate perspective.These matrices are symmetric,real,square and random,given that the underlying data generating them are random.Investigation into both the random matrices and empirical asset correlation matrices revealed that both are structurally similar in which most eigenvalues corresponding to the 'signal’ components are small, with a single large eigenvalue corresponding to the 'noise' component [18].

3.3.7Perron-Frobenius Property. The Perron-Frobenius property asserts that a real square matrixwith positive entries has a unique real eigenvalue with the largest magnitude and its corresponding eigenvector can be chosen to have strictly positive components. Correlation matrices with some negative entries were also found to have a strictly positive dominant eigenvector as well [5].While the Marchenko-Pastur distribution ensures that such a dominant eigenvalue exist, this property applies to its corresponding eigenvector components.

$$
\exists v \geqslant 0 \quad \mathrm { s . t . } \quad \rho v = \lambda _ { m a x } v ,
$$

where $\lambda _ { m a x }$ is the dominant eigenvalue, $\rho$ is the correlation matrix and $v$ is the corresponding eigenvector.The plot of the density of the dominant eigenvector component values confirms the strictly positive property by both portfolios,as shown in Figure 12.

# 3.4 Risk Management

With the statistical properties verified,we now turn to the evaluation of the application of CTS-GAN,by determining the daily Value-at-Risk (VaR)at the 90th,95th and 99th percentile for the EW portfolio of five assets.This is of interest to financial institutions as one of the corner-stones of risk-management is to minimize market overexposure without incurring opportunity costs in deploying capital. Value-at-risk,denoted by $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha }$ ,is defined as the value at which the probability of a portfolio exceeding this value is equal to $1 - \alpha$ for a given time horizon.As done previously,we simulated 10000 daily portfolio returns by multiplying the individual simulated asset returns with a vector of constant weights to form the EW portfolios.The daily $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { M C } }$ is the value at the $( 1 - \alpha )$ th-percentile of the 100oo Monte-Carlo (MC) simulations of the EW portfolios.We then calculate the daily $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } }$ based on a normality assumption on the actual historical data,using the moving window approach.

$$
\mathrm { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } } = - ( \mathbf { W } ^ { T } \pmb { \mu } + Z _ { \alpha } \sqrt { \mathbf { W } ^ { T } \Sigma \mathbf { W } } ) ,
$$

where $\mathbf { W } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { q }$ is the vector of asset weights, $\pmb { \mu } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { q }$ is the average return of the individual assets over the past trading year, $\Sigma \in \mathbb { R } ^ { q \times q }$ is the covariance matrix of the assets,and $Z _ { \alpha }$ is the $\alpha$ -quantile value ofastandardnormalrandomvariable.Wecomputethe $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { M C } }$ and the $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } }$ values for the EW portfolio returns, across $\alpha \in$ $\{ 9 0 \% , 9 5 \% , 9 9 \% \}$ .We then plot and analyze the proportion of the days for which the $\mathrm { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { i } , i \in \{ \mathrm { M C } , \mathrm { n o r m } \}$ is breached by the same portfolio of assets but with the historical returns,as well as the percentagedifencesbetween $\mathrm { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { M C } }$ and the $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } }$ values, for the days when the breached occurred.The graphs plotted are provided in Figure 13.

From the graphs,we would like to analyze the performance between VaRMC and $\mathrm { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } }$ . We look atthei) proportion ofbreaches and the ii) magnitude of those breaches,and then look at the ii) magnitude of the non-breaches,all tabulated in Table1.We would want the proportion of breaches to be low,and in the event that breaches do happen, the magnitude of those breaches should be small.For the proportion of windows in which there are no breaches, we expect that the magnitude of the non-breach,i.e. the amount bywhich the realized returns stayabove the estimated daily VaR, should be as small as possible.Tracking closer to the daily VaR estimate implies that the opportunity cost in deploying capital into the markets is lower.We find that the proportion of breaches for our model is higher than the reference normal model. This could be due to how closely our model is simulating the distribution it has learned,as was observed in Figure 3.We could treat the $\alpha = 0 . 9 5$ values of our framework to align with the 9oth percentile,as highlighted in green. Similarly,we could treat the $\alpha = 0 . 9 9$ values of our framework to align with the 95th percentile,as highlighted in orange. Being a model-free setup,and with such a pattern emerging,we are free to make such adjustments without loss of intuition. Even if the proportion of windows where the breach occurred was higher for our simulation technique,the gap is competitive at every $\alpha$ -level,meaning that the loss encountered during events where the returns were unpredictably negative,is close,between a modelbased method and model-free method.Likewise,the gap between VaRMC and actual returns is competitive with the gap between $\operatorname { V a R } _ { \alpha } ^ { \mathrm { n o r m } }$ and actual returns.This implies that our model is able to simulate just as close to the empirical returns,allowing for capital to be safely deployed. Overall,asa model-free approach,we claim that the performance of CTS-GAN is competitive at every $\alpha$ -level. The normal distribution assumption is rather consistent and does not change significantly when there is a sudden volatility in the daily returns while our framework is ultimately more reactive as it constantly changes from day to day, showing that it isable to react more quickly to volatile periods,like the COVID-19 pandemic.

# 4Conclusion

We developed a data-driven model called CTS-GAN, which incorporates conditional information into a five-network architecture setup that can capture the temporal and atemporal characteristics of the target distribution in an adversarial manner.CTS-GAN is able to generate multivariate asset prices under the condition of recently observed prices.We evaluated the quality of the synthetic data via two visualization techniques for the out-of-sample prices and returns respectively,and found strong similarity with the realized distribution.We checked that the out-of-sample prices exhibit empirically observed stylized facts.Finally,we showed an application in risk management, where we compared two sets of daily VaR values,one computed based on a normal distribution assumption, and the other using CTS-GAN,for equally-weighted portfolios.

# 4.1 Future Works

We acknowledge this work as a stepping stone for further research endeavours in this space.A deeper dive into the explainable aspects of CTS-GAN might uncover why some peculiar simulation behaviour was observed.Another important direction would be to conduct a broad comparative study with other models that can generate realistic financial time-series data and verified via stylized facts,and with appropriate metrics that summarizes the statistics for comparison purposes.Implementation research-wise,an ablation study to demonstrate the value of each of the five networks in CTS-GAN and an overall model complexity study can be conducted as well.We ran CTS-GAN at the daily time-scale and found the running-time sufficient,but a finer level of generation found in real-time applications might prove CTS-GAN limited.
