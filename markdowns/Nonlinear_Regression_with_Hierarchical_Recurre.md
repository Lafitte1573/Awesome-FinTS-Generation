# Nonlinear Regression with Hierarchical Recurrent Neural Networks Under Missing Data

S. Onur Sahin and Suleyman S. Kozat, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract—We study regression (or prediction) of sequential data, which may have missing entries and/or different lengths. This problem is heavily investigated in the machine learning literature since such missingness is a common occurrence in most real-life applications due to data corruption, measurement errors, and similar. To this end, we introduce a novel hierarchical architecture involving a set of long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, which use only the existing inputs in the sequence without any imputations or statistical assumptions on the missing data. To incorporate the missingness information, we partition the input space into different regions in a hierarchical manner based on the “presence-pattern” of the previous inputs and then assign different LSTM networks to these regions. In this sense, we use the LSTM networks as our experts for these regions and adaptively combine their outputs to generate our final output. Our method is generic so that the set of partitioned regions (presence-patterns) that are modeled by the LSTM networks can be customized and one can readily use other sequential architectures such as gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) as shown in the paper. We also provide the computational complexity analysis of the proposed architecture, which is in the same order of a conventional LSTM architecture. In our experiments, our algorithm achieves significant performance improvements on the well-known financial and real-life datasets with respect to the state-of-the-art methods. We also share the source code of our algorithm to facilitate other research and the replicability of our results.

Impact Statement—Regression on sequential data is an important and extensively studied topic with a broad range of applications in the machine learning literature. However, in most real-life applications, these data sequences usually suffer from missing data problems due to various reasons such as sensor failures or communication delays, which severally degrade the performance of most, if not all, machine learning algorithms. The proposed architecture employs different LSTM networks tuned to the specific presence patterns, therefore, inherently captures the underlying dynamics without using ad-hoc imputation methods or statistical assumptions on the data. As imputation-based methods may experience performance decreases due to the mismatch between the imputation model and the data, our algorithm does not suffer from this problem since we do not make any statistical or structural assumptions on the missing data. In our experiment results, owing to not having any such assumptions, our approach provides significant performance gains with respect to the state-of-the-art algorithms over several real-life datasets under different missing data regimes.

# I. INTRODUCTION

We investigate nonlinear regression (or prediction) of sequential time series data, where the data contains missing entries. The nonlinear processing of time series is extensively studied in the machine learning literature [1]–[4] due to its wide range of real-life applications such as fault diagnosis [5] and health care [6]. Although many highly successful algorithms are introduced for this problem [7], these algorithms are usually not designed to handle missingness and their performance significantly deteriorates in the case of missing data [8]. In this paper, we explicitly consider this missing data problem and introduce a sequential and hierarchical nonlinear learning algorithm, which effectively incorporates the missingness information by partitioning the input space into different regions based on the presence/missingness of the inputs.

Since such missing data events regularly happen in reallife applications, there exist many imputation algorithms [9]– [11] to reduce these effects. These algorithms are designed, under certain assumptions, to fill the missing entries, however, usually without directly considering the final performance. Naturally, performance of these algorithms severally degrades when these assumptions do not hold. Our algorithm has no such artificial statistical or structural assumptions on the missing data, which makes our algorithm less prone to statistical mismatches (if any). Hence, the proposed algorithm provides more stable and robust performance in different applications as demonstrated in our experiments. Specifically, we study the regression problem for variable length data sequences, which contain missing samples, in a supervised framework. In particular, we sequentially observe a data sequence along with its corresponding labels and find a nonlinear relation to predict the labels or values of the future observations.

For nonlinear regression neural network based regression methods are usually employed in real-life applications due to their capability of modeling highly complex and nonlinear structures [12]. In particular, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are usually preferred to process sequential and variable length data since they have an internal memory, which stores the past information as a state vector [12]. Although simple RNNs have a sufficient performance in learning temporal behavior and identifying sequential patterns thanks to their memory, vanishing and exploding gradient problems usually deteriorate their performance in capturing long-term relations [13]. To mitigate these problems, more advanced architectures such as the LSTM [13] and GRU [14] networks employ gating mechanisms that control the information flow in the network and provide significant performance gains. However, this performance usually degrades in the real-life applications suffering from missing data [11], [15]–[18].

To resolve the missing data problem, the most common method is to use an imputation technique for the missing samples, e.g., [9]–[11], [18]–[24], and then process the completed sequence. However, these approaches usually suffer since (i) the imputations are not adaptable, i.e., they invariably use the same pattern to substitute the missing values, or $( i i )$ an imputation drift may occur in high missingness conditions due to the accumulating mismatch between the imputed and original values. Hence, they provide less than adequate performance in real-life applications [11], [25].

We resolve these problems by introducing a sequential and hierarchical nonlinear learning algorithm based on the LSTM networks, where we treat each LSTM network as an expert in processing the inputs following a particular presence-pattern. Particularly, our architecture provides each LSTM network the capability of modeling an input sequence with a specific presence-pattern and learns to combine the outputs of these LSTM networks adaptively. By this way, the proposed algorithm incorporates the missingness information by selecting the particular LSTM networks based on the presence/missingness of a certain number of inputs. Hence, it exploits both the input signal itself as well as the presencepattern to mitigate the effects of the missing samples without making any statistical or artificial assumptions on the underlying data. In addition, our architecture keeps the computational load in terms of number of multiplication operations less than or equal to the computational load of the conventional algorithms, especially when the number of missing samples is high. Through an extensive set of experiments, we illustrate significant performance gains compared to the state-of-the-art methods in several real-life regression tasks.

Our contributions are as follows:

1) We introduce a novel hierarchical architecture based on LSTM networks for processing sequential data containing missing samples. Our architecture overcomes the missingness in the data by using specific LSTM networks instead of artificially imputing the feature vectors unlike the conventional methods [9], [10].   
2) Since the proposed architecture uses only the received data to generate its output, our algorithm prevents the deterioration of the information stored in the memory, which commonly occurs in conventional algorithms if the imputation assumptions do not hold.   
3) Our architecture can be straightforwardly applied to similar networks working in a sequential manner such as the RNN and the GRU networks.   
4) Through an extensive set of experiments involving financial and real-life datasets, we demonstrate significant performance gains achieved by our architecture with computational complexity in the order of the classical

approaches. We also share the source code1 of our algorithm to facilitate other research and the replicability of our results.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section II, we briefly review the relevant literature. We formally define our problem setting in Section III. In Section IV, we first introduce our architecture combining the LSTM networks with an example case to clarify the framework and then extend it to the generic case. In Section V, we compare the performance of our architecture with respect to the state-ofthe-art architectures. The paper concludes with several remarks in Section VI.

# II. RELATED WORKS

Many of the proposed solutions for processing sequential data containing missing samples rely on the imputation of these missing samples [9]–[11], [18]–[23]. Among these methods, [9] imputes all-zero vectors for these missing inputs. In [10], the authors use forward-filling algorithm to complete the missing data, i.e., they feed the LSTM network with the previous data when the input is missing. They also extend the input vector by a binary missingness indicator, which shows whether the input vector is originally missing or not. However, for both these methods the arbitrary/same inputs cause deterioration in the information stored in the memory of the LSTM network since the cell and the recurrent input are calculated based on these artificial input substitutions [11]. On the other hand, in our architecture the LSTM networks process only the inputs included in the data sequence based on the missingness information. Therefore, while we incorporate the missingness information, we completely preserve the content and avoid corruptions due to repetition of the same input or arbitrary data inclusions, e.g., zero values, mean values, etc., in the memory of the LSTM networks.

[11], [18]–[20], [23] utilize exponentiated negative rectifiers as the trainable decay factors considering the fading of the influence of the last observations over time. However, in these algorithms, the imputation values are restricted to the exponential-decay-based dynamics, which may show less than sufficient performance for the features that do not have these dynamics [22]. Specifically, [11] decays the last observation to the empirical mean. However, the performance may decrease in the case of multimodal, non-stationary or outlier data since the arithmetic mean may not be a robust statistic [26]. Similarly, [23] decays the last input to the observation in the previous period. However, it firstly requires data to be periodic, which may not always hold. Furthermore, the data may have multiple cycles, e.g., day, week and month, in which case each of these periods should be addressed separately. On the other hand, our architecture uses only the present input values without making any statistical assumptions on the missing values and mitigates the effects of the missing inputs by exploiting the missingness patterns instead of imputing them. Therefore, our architecture does not require data to have certain properties such as being periodic or does not suffer from the errors in modeling data by a statistics such as the mean.

[19] simultaneously learns to process the sequence and predict the next input values. Then, it imputes these predictions for the missing values. However, specifically in high missingness ratios, the error in the prediction of the next observation may increase. Hence, these errors significantly distort the information stored in the memory of the LSTM network and then accumulate through the recurrent steps. Similarly, in [21], the authors introduce a linear memory vector and predict the next input values as a linear function of the projected average of previous RNN outputs. However, the errors in the prediction of the next observations may corrupt the memory content and obstruct learning similar to [19]. On the other hand, our proposed architecture exploits only the present input values without imputing the missing ones and incorporates the missingness information by modeling missingness patterns with specific networks in a hierarchical manner. Hence, it does not suffer the problem of accumulation of errors due to inaccuracies in the prediction of missing values.

There exist other important studies for processing missing valued time series by imputing these missing entries. [22] models the underlying dynamics of the sequence by ordinary differential equations, where the decay factors using exponentiated negative rectifiers are the special case of this model. [27] first trains a Generative Adversarial Networks [28] (GAN) based model to learn the distribution of the dataset, then fix the structure and optimize the random input vector to fit the particular sequence. [29] is based on the assumption that highdimensional sequences have lower-dimensional representations with smooth dynamics in time. Based on this assumption, [29] first employs a Variational Autoencoder [30] (VAE) for dimensionality reduction and then models the dynamics in latent space with Gaussian Processes. However, these methods may show less than sufficient performance when the statistical assumptions do not hold.

In addition to the methods based on the imputation of missing samples, [31] and [32] are originally designed to process non-uniformly sampled sequential data. [31] extends the classical LSTM architecture with a time gate to incorporate the nonlinear effect of the time difference between observations. [32] learns a periodic sampler function, which is defined by period, shift and on-ratio parameters, and processes only a small portion of the input sequence sampled by this function. Another noteworthy direction is to handle missing input values in the test phase while the training set is complete as in [33]. The authors in [33] robustify the network against the missing data by enforcing missingness on the inputs in the training phase, i.e., [33] randomly deletes a portion of input values in the training phase by applying the Dropout [34] mechanism on the input. [25] constructs a Graph Neural Network [35] to process data sequences suffering from missing entries, where each node represents a feature and the edges indicate the relations between these features. The authors in [36] state that the “sequentialness prior” may harm the performance in certain cases; therefore, [36] treats the time series as the sets to cancel the “sequentialness prior” and process the whole sequence at once by the sum of set functions. They use a variant of positional encodings [37] with trigonometric functions to incorporate the time information. These approaches are completely orthogonal to our contribution and can be incorporated into our architecture straightforwardly. Also, the missingness information they use in the training phase may not match the missingness in the test data due to non-stationarity and similar.

Although they are not directly focused on the missing data problem, there exist noteworthy studies [38]–[45] optimizing the connections between the LSTM networks and showing performance improvements as in our architecture. Specifically, [38]–[41] stack LSTM networks to increase the modeling capacity, while [42]–[44] modify flow of the information in the LSTM networks. These LSTM architectures in [38]–[44] can be straightforwardly incorporated into the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture as the individual LSTM networks. Furthermore, [45] models the movement of pedestrians by separate LSTM networks and shares information using a pooling mechanism between these LSTM networks similar to our architecture. Our architecture can be directly used in [45] in the case of missing data. In addition, the LSTM networks on the hierarchical network topologies show performance improvements compared to the classical LSTM networks [46]–[48]. In our architecture, we introduce a novel hierarchical LSTM architecture, which specifically incorporates the missingness information by assigning specific LSTM networks to each missingness pattern.

The conventional LSTM-based methods in [9], [10], [19], [20], [23] provide less than adequate performance to process sequential data containing missing samples since they suffer from certain obstacles such as deterioration in the information stored in the memory and accumulation of the errors in the prediction of the missing values. Here, we employ a novel LSTM network based on a hierarchical architecture, which combines the outputs of a variable number of LSTM networks for sequential regression tasks “without” sacrificing computational load. Our architecture assigns a specific LSTM network to the underlying sequence based on the missingness information and incorporates this missingness information without imputing the missing values.

Here, the vectors in $\boldsymbol { X }$ are expected to be received at a constant rate, however, $\boldsymbol { X }$ contains missing samples, i.e., certain input vectors, $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } }$ , are corrupted or missing from the data sequence. The sequence of present vectors is denoted by $X ^ { + }$ . Note that $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ is either completely received or completely missing, i.e., we do not consider the case that only certain entries of $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } }$ are missing.

The desired output for the regression vector $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ is given by $\ b { y } _ { t _ { k } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { u }$ and our goal is to estimate $\boldsymbol { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ by

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \hat { \pmb { y } } _ { t _ { k } } = f _ { t _ { k } } \big ( \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k } } , \ldots , \pmb { x } _ { t _ { 1 } } , \pmb { y } _ { t _ { k - 1 } } , \ldots , \pmb { y } _ { t _ { 1 } } \big ) , } \end{array}
$$

where $f _ { t _ { k } } ( \cdot )$ is a possibly time-varying and adaptive nonlinear regression function at time step $t _ { k }$ . The estimate $\hat { \pmb y } _ { t _ { k } }$ is a function of the current and past observations. Note that in certain tasks such as the next value prediction problem $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ is not only the output, but also the next input, i.e., $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } } = \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k + 1 } }$ In such cases, either one of $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ or $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ may be missing, whereas the other one exists. For the tasks where $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ is only the output of $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ , i.e., $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } } \neq \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k + 1 } }$ , either both $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ and $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ are received or both are missing. Similar to $X ^ { + }$ , the sequence of present desired outputs is denoted by $Y ^ { + }$ . For the input $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } }$ , the incurred loss is $l ( \boldsymbol { y } _ { t _ { k } } , \hat { \boldsymbol { y } } _ { t _ { k } } )$ , if $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } }$ exists, and for the whole vector sequence $\boldsymbol { X }$ , we suffer

$$

$$

where $n ^ { + }$ is the number of present desired outputs and $\mathbb { 1 }$ is the indicator function, i.e., $\mathbb { 1 } _ { Y ^ { + } } ( \pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } } ) = 1$ if $\pmb { y } _ { t _ { k } } \in \pmb { Y } ^ { + }$ and 0 otherwise.

To clarify the framework, in Fig. 1, we illustrate an example data sequence $\textbf { \textit { X } } = \ [ x _ { t _ { 1 } } , . . . , x _ { t _ { 1 1 } } ]$ , where each vector is selected as a scalar, i.e., $m = 1$ , with constant time intervals $\delta$ . However, the data sequence has missing samples, i.e., $x _ { t _ { 4 } }$ , $x _ { t _ { 7 } }$ and ${ \boldsymbol { x } } _ { t _ { 8 } }$ . Since the time intervals between the receiving consecutive regression vectors may not be regular, the regression function should adapt to different cases to predict the desired signal. As an example, let us consider one-step-ahead prediction as a special regression task for this input sequence. Then, $\boldsymbol { x } _ { t _ { 3 } }$ should be predicted using the $\boldsymbol { x } _ { t _ { 1 } }$ and $x _ { t _ { 2 } }$ . # IV. LSTM NETWORK BASED HIERARCHICAL ARCHITECTURE

Since the time intervals between the present input vectors are not regular in the case of missing data, the regression function should adapt to different scenarios to estimate the desired signal. Hence, we directly incorporate the missingness information into our nonlinear regression function $f _ { t _ { k } } ( \cdot )$ to model the effect of the missing data in our sequence. In our algorithm, we consider the missing input values in a particular window, which shifts at each time step. The length of this window is a user-defined parameter, controls the modeling capacity and optimized by cross-validation. We investigate effects of window length in Section V, along with other parameters.

For this purpose, we first partition the regression function $f _ { t _ { k } } ( \cdot )$ into two parts as follows

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \hat { \pmb { y } } _ { t _ { k } } = \alpha _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot ) + \alpha _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot ) , } \end{array}
$$

where $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot )$ processes the input sequence in a particular window, e.g., the length-3 window in Fig. 1, and $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot )$ is the main regression function using the whole input sequence except the samples inside the window. The purpose of two distinct functions will be clear in the following.

Specifically, for a length- $L$ window, $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot )$ and $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot )$ process the inputs $[ \pmb { x } _ { t _ { 1 } } , \dots , \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k - L } } ]$ and $[ \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k - L + 1 } } , \dots , \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k } } ]$ , respectively. Here, $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot )$ captures the general pattern of the data, while $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot )$ provides more elaborate decisions on the inputs inside the window. Next, we incorporate the missingness information in the length- $L$ window into $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot )$ .

We define “presence-pattern”, i.e., the pattern of the presence of the input samples, π(L)tk $\pi _ { t _ { k } } ^ { ( L ) } ~ = ~ [ \pi _ { t _ { k } , 1 } ^ { \dot { ( } L ) } , \dots , \pi _ { t _ { k } , L } ^ { ( L ) } ] ^ { \dot { T } } ~ \in$ $\{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { L }$ , which holds the missingness information in its most explicit form, i.e., whether the inputs $[ \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k - L + 1 } } , \dots , \pmb { x } _ { t _ { k } } ]$ exist or not. For example, a length-3 presence-pattern $\pi _ { t _ { k } } ^ { ( 3 ) } ~ =$ $[ 1 , 0 , 1 ] ^ { T }$ indicates that $\mathbf { \Delta } \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k - 2 } }$ and $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } } }$ are received, however, $\mathbf { \mathcal { x } } _ { t _ { k - 1 } }$ is missing from the input sequence. The presencepattern always has the same length with the window, i.e., $L = W$ , hence, we drop the length $L$ to simplify the notation, i.e., $\pi _ { t _ { k } }$ . Note that we explicitly incorporate the missingness information into $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { W } } ( \cdot , \pi _ { t _ { k } } )$ , besides, $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot )$ carries this information for the past inputs thanks to the memory in the LSTM architecture. Hence, we also provide this missingness information for the previous inputs.

For an $L$ length window, naturally, there may exist $2 ^ { L }$ different presence-patterns, i.e., each input vector may or may not be present. Since all-zero presence-pattern indicates all of the inputs inside the window are missing, we have $2 ^ { L } - 1$ presence-patterns. In our algorithm, we assign a unique regression function to process each of these patterns, while $f _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \mathrm { M } } ( \cdot )$ corresponds to all-zero pattern since it only uses the inputs outside the window, which is the main reason using two functions in (11).

By this way, we directly incorporate the missingness information by selecting the particular leaf LSTM networks instead of artificially inserting it into the input vectors as done in the literature [9], [10]. Due to this hierarchical nature, we name our architecture as the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture.

Remark 1: We introduce the most extensive variant of the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture, i.e., all of the $2 ^ { L }$ LSTM networks are included. Since we provide an adaptive combination algorithm working on any number of LSTM networks, one can use only a desired set of LSTM networks by exclusively altering the set $\Pi _ { t _ { k } }$ . As an example, for the length-3 input presence-pattern $\pi _ { t _ { k } } ^ { \ddot { } } = [ 1 , 1 , 1 ] ^ { T }$ , one can employ only the LSTM networks with the presence-patterns $[ 1 , 0 , 0 ] ^ { T }$ , $[ 1 , \dot { 1 } , 0 ] ^ { T }$ and $[ 1 , 1 , 1 ] ^ { T }$ instead of the $2 ^ { 3 } = 8$ LSTM networks, which corresponds to the combination of $1 - , 2 -$ and 3−step ahead predictors. Therefore, the number of LSTM networks can be reduced to avoid overfitting issues and accelerate the training of the architecture thanks to our adaptive combination algorithm. Furthermore, we can employ different update schemes to train these LSTM networks, e.g., dynamic learning rate for the LSTM networks based on the occurrence frequencies of their presence-patterns. In addition, one can also use a common weight vector $\tilde { w }$ to calculate λ(i)tk ’s for all of the LSTM networks instead of assigning a unique weight vector $\tilde { \mathbf { \pmb { w } } } ^ { ( i ) }$ for each of them.

Remark 2: We point out that our Hierarchical-LSTM architecture can be straightforwardly extended as follows. i) One can combine multiple Hierarchical-LSTM networks with distinct window lengths in the sense of the combination of a mixture of experts. ii) One can start with a small window length, e.g., $L \ = \ 1$ , and then increase this window length as the leaf LSTM networks are trained. We note that these trained leaf LSTM networks constitute half of the leaf LSTM networks when we increase the window length by 1, i.e., the leaf LSTM networks containing a 0 in the first entry of their presence-patterns. By this way, the capacity of the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture incrementally grows as we increase the window length. In addition, since half of the leaf LSTM networks start with substantially trained weights, the trainability characteristics of the architecture for the large window lengths may significantly increase.

RITSI [19] is the LSTM network employing uncorrelated recurrent imputation for the missing values.

In the vanilla LSTM architecture, there exist four matrixvector multiplications for the input, i.e., $\mathbf { \Delta } W \mathbf { x } _ { t _ { k } }$ , four matrixvector multiplications for the recurrent input, i.e., $\boldsymbol { U } \boldsymbol { s } _ { t _ { k - 1 } }$ , and three vector-vector multiplications between the gates, i.e., (7) and (8), which correspond to $4 q ^ { 2 } + 4 q m + 3 q$ multiplication operations in total. Since the LSTM-FI algorithm extends the feature vector with a binary missingness indicator, the input size is $m + 1$ for this algorithm, which requires 4 additional multiplication operations, i.e., $4 q ^ { 2 } + 4 q m + 7 q$ . The RITSI algorithm has an additional matrix-vector multiplication, i.e., $R s _ { t _ { k - 1 } }$ , where $\pmb { R } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { m \times q }$ , to estimate the missing values, which requires $q m$ additional multiplication operations. Hence, the total number of multiplication operations in the RITSI algorithm is $4 q ^ { 2 } + 5 q m + 3 q$ .

Since the LSTM-ZI, LSTM-FI and RITSI algorithms impute the missing inputs with the all-zero vectors, the previous existing input vectors and the recurrent prediction, respectively, these algorithms require $N$ LSTM operations to process a length- $N$ sequence. On the other hand, the HierarchicalLSTM architecture processes only the existing inputs in the sequence, which requires $N - M$ Hierarchical-LSTM network operations to process the same sequence. For each step of the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture, the main LSTM network processes each existing input once, however, the number of active leaf LSTM alters based on the presence-pattern of the input. For a length- $L$ window, there exist $2 ^ { L }$ different leaf LSTM networks, which require $2 ^ { L } \times \frac { L } { 2 }$ LSTM operations in total if all $L$ inputs in the window exist. If one input is missing, the total number of LSTM operations for this window decreases to $\begin{array} { r } { 2 ^ { L - 1 } \times \frac { L - 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ . Similarly, the total number of LSTM operations for this window is $2 ^ { L - 2 } \times \frac { L - 2 } { 2 }$ L−2 for the case two inputs are missing. We emphasize that the computational load of the Hierarchical-LSTM architecture depends on the distribution of the missing inputs in addition to number of missing inputs. In the worst (also unrealistic) case, the missing and existing inputs are completely separated, the total computational load for our architecture is $( N - M ) ( 1 +$ $2 ^ { L - 1 } L ) ( 4 q ^ { 2 } + 4 q m + 3 q )$ . # VI. CONCLUSION

We investigated nonlinear regression of variable length sequential data, where the data suffers from missing inputs. We introduce the Hierarchical-LSTM network, which is a novel hierarchical architecture based on the LSTM networks. The Hierarchical-LSTM architecture contains a set of LSTM networks, where each LSTM network is trained as an expert for processing the inputs following a particular presencepattern, i.e., we partition the input space into subspaces in a hierarchical manner based on the presence-patterns and assign specific LSTM networks to these subpatterns. We adaptively combine the outputs of these LSTM networks based on the presence-pattern and construct the final output at each time step. The introduced algorithm protects the LSTM networks against performance losses due to $i$ ) statistical mismatches commonly faced by the widely used imputation methods and $\romannumeral 2$ ) imputation drift, since our architecture uses only the existing inputs without any assumption on the missing data. In addition, the computational load of our algorithm is less than the computational load of the conventional algorithms in terms of the number of multiplication operations particularly under high missingness ratios. We emphasize that our architecture can be readily applied to the other recurrent architectures such as the RNNs and GRU networks. The Hierarchical-LSTM network demonstrates significant performance improvements with respect to the state-of-the-art methods in several different well-known real-life and financial datasets. We also openly share the source code of our algorithm to facilitate other studies and for the reproducibility of our results. Future work may explore the selection of a subset of presence-patterns instead of using all presence-patterns so that one can use Hierarchical-LSTM architecture with large window lengths by keeping the number of parameters and the computational load in the same level.
