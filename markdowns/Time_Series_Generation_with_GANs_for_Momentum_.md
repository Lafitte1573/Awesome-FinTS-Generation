# Time Series Generation with GANs for Momentum Effect Simulation on Moscow Stock Exchange

Maksim Kazadaev Faculty of Computer Science, National Research University Higher School of Economics mskazadaev@edu.hse.ru Moscow, Russia

Vitaliy Pozdnyakov, Ilya Makarov AIRI pozdnyakov@airi.net, makarov@airi.net Moscow, Russia

Abstract—The ability to accurately simulate financial markets is crucial, as it allows researchers and practitioners to rigorously test and refine trading strategies without the high risks associated with real-world experimentation. By leveraging Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), this research aims to enhance the robustness and effectiveness of trading strategies by providing a controlled environment to assess potential outcomes and strategy resilience under varied market conditions.

In this work, we propose the application of GANs for simulating multidimensional time series in the context of developing and testing trading strategies. We conduct an experimental study to jointly simulate the log-returns of several stocks on Moscow Exchange for Momentum effect evaluation. Compared to traditional methods such as bootstrapping, GANs can better model and interpolate the non-parametric complex nature of the data, providing an increased diverse sample size. This methodology could be beneficial for investors seeking new opportunities to test and tune hyperparameters of other trading strategies.

Keywords—Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN), Momentum Effect.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Financial time series are inherently noisy and present a substantial challenge for predictive modeling, compounded by the diversity in levels of data aggregation. Trading data can be segmented into various granularities: from high-frequency order book and transaction flow data, to intraday price points capturing the last transaction of each minute, and up to daily price summaries typically utilized in portfolio strategies. Each level of aggregation embodies unique noise characteristics and data availability issues, influencing the strategy development process [1].

The development of trading strategies often begins with the identification of a trading idea, followed by the formulation of a strategy that includes various hyperparameters. These parameters are traditionally optimized using historical data. However, the inherent noise in financial time series can lead to model overfitting, particularly in portfolio strategies where the daily data frequency severely limits the available sample size, making the overfitting problem worse [2].

To address these challenges, bootstrapping has been employed to simulate more data samples from a limited dataset.

Despite its usefulness, bootstrapping has limitations, especially when the original sample is small, potentially leading to overfitted trading strategies that perform well on historical data but poorly on unseen data [3].

GANs offer a solution to these limitations by generating synthetic financial time series data. This capability allows for more effective and expansive testing and tuning of trading strategies. Synthetic datasets generated by GANs can simulate longer time periods [4] and offer varied market scenarios, providing a robust platform for strategy development and evaluation [5], [6]. By using GANs, we can potentially reduce variance in estimates and avoid overfitting, which is a common problem when training models on small samples. Generative models can interpolate the distribution of log-returns, helping to mitigate overfitting to real data.

We choose to model the Momentum strategy because it operates with complex temporal multidimensional dependencies in time series data. The Momentum strategy is based on the observation that stocks which have performed well in the past tend to continue performing well in the near future. This phenomenon is often attributed to investor psychology where past winners attract more attention and investment, potentially driving their prices up further [7]. Such behavior is not entirely rational but is commonly observed in emerging markets [8], making it a good strategy to analyze on the Moscow Stock Exchange.

In this paper we propose a methodology to simulate multidimensional financial time series using GANs. We integrate TCN architectures into both the generator and discriminator components of our GANs and show that it is able to effectively capture temporal and cross-sectional dependencies. Beyond generating data, we demonstrate how these synthetic datasets can be used for backtesting and evaluating the Momentum effect, which is our primary contribution.

Our contributions are as follows:

• We established a robust training methodology that ensures our GANs are well-suited for generating high-fidelity synthetic data.

• We performed an in-depth analysis to evaluate how closely the characteristics of the generated data align with those of actual financial data. • Our evaluations showed that our GAN models successfully reproduce simple correlations found in real data, however, they may fail to capture weak dependencies such as those related to the Momentum effect.

Section VIII concludes this work, summarizing our findings and discussing potential avenues for future research. # II. RELATED WORK

Generative models have been prominently used in the fields of image and text generation, but their application in the financial trading sector is relatively recent. One of the initial uses of these models in trading has been for the fine-tuning of strategy parameters. Koshiyama et al. [3] demonstrated how GANs can be used to optimize hyperparameters across multiple trading strategies. Their research highlighted that strategies fine-tuned with GAN-generated samples tend to exhibit increased stability and robustness.

Another significant application of GANs in this field is in the direct training of predictive models on synthetic data. Carvajal et al. [6] highlighted how synthetic data augmentations can serve as a form of model regularization, enhancing the stability of the strategies developed due to the inherently noisy nature of financial data.

Backtesting is another critical area where GANs have proven beneficial. Lezmi et al. [5] showed that using synthetic data for backtesting allow more accurate estimation of the distribution of strategy returns over extended periods.

In the realm of high-frequency trading, Li et al. [9] introduced an innovative approach by directly simulating orders in the order book without aggregation, highlighting its potential utility in evaluating strategy’s positions in the order queue. Following this, Coletta et al. [10] addressed the impact of large trades on order book dynamics, a key concern in market impact estimations.

The work by Cont et al. [11] focused on evaluating the quality of synthetic market data. They proposed a set of stylized facts to gauge the similarity between generated data and actual market data, establishing a foundation for assessing the effectiveness of generative models in trading applications.

Study [4] also highlighted the statistical properties comparison between the generated and real market data focusing on heavy tails of the returns distribution.

The discriminator component of the Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) architecture has also been explored for applications such as anomaly detection, as detailed by Kim et al. [12]. This capability is particularly valuable in trading, where it can identify discrepancies or anomalies in market conditions that deviate from a trading strategy’s assumptions, potentially preventing the execution of trades in unfavorable environments.

The study by He et al. [13] explored the potential of GANs for predicting future stock returns, demonstrating that GANs, through their generator component, can outperform simpler predictive models. The authors attributed this enhanced performance to improved regularization, facilitated by the inherent randomness in the generator’s input, which contributes to more robust and generalizable model predictions.

This work builds upon these foundations, employing GANs based on Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) to model multidimensional series of returns for Momentum effect evaluation. Our approach not only measures the quality of the generated data but also underscores the practical utility of GAN-generated data in optimizing and validating trading strategies.

# III. GAN ARCHITECTURE

We consider the problem of developing and testing trading strategies on generated time series data. The primary goal is to create a robust model that can simulate realistic financial time series to serve as a testing ground for various trading strategies, particularly those exploiting the Momentum strategy. The challenge lies in accurately capturing the complex, nonlinear dependencies and temporal dynamics characteristic of financial markets.

To address this challenge, we selected the TCN as our core architecture for both the generator and discriminator components of our GANs. TCNs are particularly well-suited for GANs training due to their learning stability and ability to capture long-term dependencies using dilated convolutions. The efficacy of TCNs in similar tasks for returns generation is well-documented in [4]. This precedence validates our choice, suggesting that TCNs can effectively model the temporal correlations commonly observed in financial time series. One major constraint is the receptive field, which is limited by the depth and dilation rates of the convolutions. This means that TCNs might struggle with extremely long-range dependencies unless carefully configured, which can lead to increased computational complexity and training times.

We use a window size of 126. The noise dimension for the generator is three times the number of assets, i.e., 15. Multiplying by the window size, we obtain $1 2 6 \cdot 1 5$ random noise values that the generator takes as an input.

In our model, both the discriminator and the generator share the same structural design. Initially, we use a convolution with a kernel size of 1 to adjust the input to match the number of hidden channels, which can be up to 10. This is followed by a series of four convolutional layers, each with a kernel size of 2 and increasing dilation and padding rates of 1, 2, 4, and 8, respectively, which allow the network to expand its receptive field exponentially. The output of each of these layers is truncated from the right side to keep the output size consistent across layers and to prevent using the future noise values for each time.

The outputs of these layers are stored and later combined through skip connections, taking advantage of their uniform sizes to facilitate this addition. The final convolutional layer changes the dimensionality to match the number of assets for the generator, or reduces it to a single dimension for the discriminator. For the discriminator, this output then passes through a linear layer followed by a sigmoid activation to produce a probability.

A critical aspect of our approach involves the consistent use of a specific set of noise values across all data points. We utilize a moving window strategy that captures the last 126 noise values, ensuring continuity and similarity between adjacent time periods. This method prevents the model from incorporating future noise values in the current observation’s generation. Such a technique is essential for simulating correlated historical sequences, which is crucial for modeling features like volatility clustering, the Momentum effect, and other temporal dependencies in financial time series data.

# IV. EXPERIMENTS WITH GAN

In the training of the GAN, a comprehensive historical dataset is essential. Therefore, this study focused on five stocks from the Moscow Exchange that provided the long data history, approximately ten years. To validate the GAN’s capability in detecting correlations between time series, we selected stocks within the same industry, specifically the banking sector. For instance, SBERP and SBER, which represent the same company, were chosen because their returns are expected to be highly correlated. Additionally, the returns between SBER and another banking company, VTBR, were also analyzed due to their operational similarities within the same industry. This approach aims to establish the effectiveness of GANs in recognizing and learning from these financial correlations.

It is crucial to acknowledge that selecting stocks with the most extensive historical data introduces a selection bias into our strategies. Stocks with longer histories are typically those that have shown growth and sustainability, avoiding bankruptcy. This bias can influence the robustness and applicability of the trading strategies.

The following 5 stocks were selected:

• SBER – Sberbank (banking)   
• SBERP – Sberbank preferred shares (banking)   
• VTBR – VTB Bank (banking)   
• LKOH – Lukoil (oil)   
• MOEX – Moscow Exchange (financial services)

Daily log-returns for approximately 10 years were calculated for these stocks, and we simulated the behavior of these five time series.

For training, a batch size of 256 (sampling 256 random continuous segments of length 126) was used along with the RMSprop optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0002.

During training, the GAN exhibited unstable results during the generation phase, necessitating two modifications:

1) Clipping the weights of the discriminator to the range [-0.01, 0.01] to prevent them from becoming too large. This helps to mitigate the poor quality of neural networks with large weights.   
2) Training the discriminator on each batch and the generator on every second batch. This approach extends the training time but results in more stable learning: after these modifications, the generated data became more realistic.

We trained for 800 epochs. The training took approximately 5-10 minutes on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 Ti GPU.

During generation, we normalize the output so that the average returns and their standard deviations for each stock match those in the real series over the entire time period. Thus, the GAN simulates dependencies between stock returns, not just their averages and standard deviations over the interval.

We generate time series using the GAN and studied its properties. As shown in Figure 1, similar to real returns, stocks tend to rise and fall simultaneously. However, unlike the original time series, which experienced a significant spike in 2022, the fake series does not have such large spikes. However, there are occasional drops in 2016 in fake data nearly as severe as in 2020 in the real data. Excluding the 2022 outlier, our model appears capable of crisis modelling.

# V. MOMENTUM STRATEGY

The Momentum strategy involves investing a larger share of funds in stocks that have recently been on the rise. We have chosen this strategy because the Momentum effect is widely observed among the emerging markets [8]. The motivation behind this strategy is that people are not rational and tend to invest more in stocks that have grown in the past, thereby stimulating their future growth. There are other arguments for this strategy, that can be found in the relevant literature [7]. We introduce the following metric at each point in time for each stock:

$$
\mathbf { M } _ { t } = \sum _ { i = n _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } } ^ { n _ { \mathrm { f i n i s h } } } r _ { t - i } ,
$$

where $r _ { t }$ is the return of the stock at time $t$ and $n _ { \mathrm { f i n i s h } } > n _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } }$ are hyperparameters. This metric indicates how much the stock has grown recently. The larger $n _ { \mathrm { f i n i s h } }$ and $n _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } }$ , the more distant returns we consider. The greater the distance between these parameters, the larger the number of past returns we take into account.

At each point in time, we sort 5 stocks by the metric $M _ { t }$ and assign them numbers from 0 to 4. Then, we invest funds in all stocks proportionally to their number in the sorted list (the stock with the highest $M _ { t }$ receives the largest share, while the stock with the lowest $M _ { t }$ receives no investment).

The objective for a strategy can be defined in various ways. A naive approach is to maximize the expected annual return. In reality, investors are concerned not only with expected returns but also with the variance of those returns. Since investors tend to avoid risk, the standard deviation of returns should also be considered in the strategy’s evaluation.

Instead of maximizing expected returns, we maximize the Sharpe Ratio (SR), which indicates the return-to-risk ratio:

![](images/c174130e678aa8a114bfb1429e284191b6f40b2a2eb5a438eaba2a1976366545.jpg)  
Fig. 3: Distribution of real and fake returns. Dashed lines indicate maximum and minimum returns. The 2022 outlier was excluded from the real data for this analysis.

$$
\mathrm { S R } = \frac { \mathbb { E } [ R _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } } - R _ { f } ] } { \sqrt { \mathbb { D } [ R _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } } - R _ { f } ] } } = \frac { \mathbb { E } [ R _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } } - R _ { f } ] } { \sigma _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } } } ,
$$

where $R _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } }$ is the annual return of the strategy, $R _ { f }$ is the risk-free rate, for instance, return from government bonds, and $\sigma _ { \mathrm { s t r a t e g y } }$ is the standard deviation of the strategy’s annual returns.

When calculating the Sharpe Ratio, it should be considered that the risk-free return can change over time, and there are many different techniques for its calculation. For simplification, in this work, the risk-free return is assumed to be $0 \%$ . This approximation does not hinder the comparison of the derived strategies.

# VI. BACKTESTING THE MOMENTUM STRATEGY

The reference point for comparison with the Momentum strategy is the uniform strategy. The uniform strategy assigns an equal weight to each stock within the portfolio and is a common baseline to compare with.

Let us fix $n _ { \mathrm { f i n i s h } } = 3 6 5$ and $n _ { \mathrm { s t a r t } } = 1 8 0$ . At each point in time, we calculate the return in the following period for the stock that ranked $i$ -th according to the metric (1). For each rank $i$ , we calculate the average annual return and its $9 5 \%$ confidence interval. The results on real data are presented in Figure 4. It can be observed that the returns of stocks for which (1) is higher are, on average, greater. However, the variance of returns is very large, and we cannot assert that the strategy works at a high level of confidence.

Figure 6 shows the cumulative returns of the strategy on real and fake data. It can be seen that this strategy significantly drops during crises. The results for real returns and 100 fake return histories are presented in Table I. Nevertheless, the average annual return of the Momentum strategy on fake data is not significantly higher than that of the uniform strategy. # VIII. CONCLUSION

In this study, we have made significant advancements in simulating financial markets using GANs. The research began with the collection of a dataset consisting of returns from Russian stocks over the past decade, which was essential for training our neural network models. We then successfully implemented Temporal Convolutional Network architectures for both the generator and discriminator components of our GANs and developed a comprehensive training pipeline. Through these models, we generated fake data and conducted thorough analysis to compare its characteristics with those of real financial data.

Our findings indicate that the GANs were capable of capturing simple dependencies within the data, such as correlations between similar stocks, with a high degree of fidelity to actual data. However, the models were less successful in identifying and reproducing more complex and noisy dependencies, such as those associated with the Momentum effect in stock returns. Despite these limitations, the proposed TCN architectures hold promise for applications in risk management by effectively capturing correlations between returns and simulating conditions akin to financial crises.

Looking to the future, there are several ways to further expand the capabilities of our GANs. One immediate step would be to adjust the returns for market neutrality by integrating the CAPM model to minimize the influence of overall market movements, allowing the models to detect subtler patterns in the data. Extending the application of our methods to other asset classes, such as U.S. stocks or cryptocurrencies, could provide a richer dataset and potentially enhance model performance. Additionally, employing data from shorter time intervals, such as hourly or minute-level data, could furnish more training examples and further refine the model’s accuracy. Exploring alternative neural network architectures, such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks for GANs, and testing other strategic frameworks like the Markowitz model for portfolio optimization, could provide improvements in the simulation. Additional experiments could compare the GAN’s performance across different market conditions and with different types of financial instruments. Incorporating a broader range of market conditions into future experiments, including testing models during extreme market events, such as financial crises or periods of high volatility, will help evaluate their robustness and adaptability.
